## Abstract

Gérard Debreu's prize recognized rigorous reformulation of general equilibrium theory and contributions to understanding the price system. His work asks when individually chosen plans can fit together across markets and what properties the resulting allocation has. The contribution is not a proof that an actual economy always reaches a unique, efficient outcome. It is a framework that makes the conditions and logical scope of such statements explicit. The academy emphasized both new analytical techniques and the distinction between a theory and its interpretation. [@ceremony-1983]

## In plain English

Imagine every household making a shopping list and every producer making a production plan. Prices affect those plans, but the plans must also be mutually compatible. Households cannot collectively consume more of a good than is available, and producers cannot all use inputs that nobody supplies.

General equilibrium asks whether there are prices at which the decentralized plans agree. Showing that such prices exist is a consistency question. Asking whether they are unique, whether trading will find them or whether the resulting allocation is fair introduces different questions.

Debreu's mathematical approach helps keep those questions separate. A theorem can explain exactly what a model establishes and reveal which assumptions support it. That precision provides a starting point for empirical evaluation; it is not itself evidence that the world satisfies the assumptions. The academy explicitly warns against reading the efficiency results as an automatic recommendation for laissez-faire. [@ceremony-1983]

## The problem before this work

Walras formulated a broad system linking demands, supplies and prices. Counting equations and unknowns, however, cannot guarantee a solution with meaningful quantities. Nor does a plausible economic story establish that all the mathematical conditions needed for consistency have been met.

The academy situates Debreu's work after earlier existence research, including Abraham Wald, and explicitly credits Lionel McKenzie's concurrent contributions alongside Arrow and Debreu. The history is collective even when an award recognizes one researcher. [@ceremony-1983]

Abstraction posed a second challenge. Consumers and producers need not share one convenient differentiable formula. A general theory needs ways to describe feasible sets and preferences without building every result into a special example. Debreu's lecture introduces a commodity space and the mathematical representation of economic actions, explaining the appeal of vector-space methods. [@debreu-lecture-1983]

## What the laureates contributed

**Debreu was the sole recipient this year.** With Kenneth Arrow, he developed the 1954 equilibrium-existence paper for an integrated economy of production, exchange and consumption. Later work broadened the theory's conditions and investigated additional properties. The academy also credits his contribution to welfare analysis and the extension of the framework to uncertainty and other applications. [@ceremony-1983]

The 1954 paper must be described accurately. On page 266 the authors explicitly state that uniqueness and stability are not investigated. Existence is enough to establish that the model's plans can be consistent under its assumptions; it is not a demonstration of the dynamics by which a market would reach that state. [@arrow-debreu-1954]

Debreu also helped raise standards for economic argument. Defining the commodity space, feasible production sets and preference properties allows readers to identify what is being assumed rather than infer it from an appealing diagram. His lecture describes rigor, generalization, simplification and extension as interconnected research goals. [@debreu-lecture-1983]

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented exchange economy with two people and two goods. Person A initially owns two apples and no bread; person B owns two loaves and no apples. Both value a balanced basket according to $u(a,b)=ab$, where $a$ and $b$ denote quantities.

At prices of one unit of money for either good, each person's endowment is worth two. Spending one on each good gives a basket of one apple and one loaf. Both choose that basket, and aggregate consumption of two of each good matches the available resources.

Now try an apple price of two and a bread price of one. A's endowment is worth four and B's two. With these preferences, each spends half their income on each good. Together they demand 1.5 apples and three loaves. That is inconsistent with the available two of each. These trial prices do not clear both markets.

This original illustration finds an equilibrium in one simple economy. It does not prove an existence theorem for every economy or describe a real adjustment process. Different preferences, resources or restrictions can change the result, and the utility formula is chosen for clarity rather than estimated from data.

## The core economics

A household's choice in this exchange example solves

$$
\max_{a,b\geq0} ab\quad\text{subject to}\quad p_a a+p_b b\leq m.
$$

$p_a$ and $p_b$ are prices and $m$ is the value of that household's initial endowment. For positive income and prices, the optimal quantities are

$$
a=\frac{m}{2p_a},\qquad b=\frac{m}{2p_b}.
$$

At equal prices the two budgets and demands produce the clearing allocation described above. Multiplying both prices by the same positive number changes neither relative opportunities nor real demands. A nominal normalization therefore does not by itself create economic uniqueness.

The general framework replaces this special utility formula with assumptions on preferences and feasible sets. Convexity, continuity and conditions supporting demand and feasible resource use are substantive elements of existence results. Different theorems impose different precise conditions; this page does not claim that the example substitutes for their proofs. [@arrow-debreu-1954]

A commodity must also be defined carefully. A delivery at one place or date is not automatically the same economic good as delivery elsewhere or later. Representing such distinctions makes the model more explicit, while also showing how demanding a complete set of markets can be. [@arrow-debreu-1954] [@debreu-lecture-1983]

## What changed as a result

Debreu's framework supplied a general language for examining coordination and the conditions behind efficiency. The academy describes applications ranging from uncertainty and public-sector questions to environmental and economy-wide analysis. Those applications need additional modeling assumptions; they do not inherit empirical accuracy automatically from an abstract theorem. [@ceremony-1983]

For readers, the central benefit is disciplined interpretation. A result can be logically correct and still unsuitable for a particular empirical question. Stating the assumptions clearly makes that limitation visible instead of disguising it as a failure of mathematics.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

An equilibrium-existence result leaves adjustment, information and institutional implementation open. Indivisible choices, increasing returns or missing markets may require other tools or materially change the conclusions. Efficiency also does not determine a fair distribution of initial resources.

The example deliberately assumes complete knowledge and frictionless trade. Its gains demonstrate possibilities inside that model, not a causal prediction for a policy reform. Fuller original-text consultation, a more detailed account of later equilibrium research and independent review remain necessary before this draft can be treated as a finished scholarly explanation.

## Key takeaways

- Compatible individual plans require more than sensible choices in isolation.
- Existence, uniqueness, stability and fairness remain different questions.
- Mathematical assumptions shape economic conclusions.
- Prices are relative signals; a nominal normalization is not a uniqueness proof.
- Debreu's contribution includes analytical methods and standards of argument.
