## Abstract

Theodore W. Schultz and Sir Arthur Lewis shared the prize for development research, particularly concerning poorer countries. Schultz emphasized investment in people's skills and health, agricultural productivity and the incentives faced by farmers. Lewis developed a model of expansion in a capitalist sector supplied by labor from lower-productivity activities, linking profits, reinvestment and growth. Their approaches ask different questions and need not give the same diagnosis of a particular economy. Neither treats poverty as evidence that poor people lack economic motives, and neither supplies a universally applicable industrialization recipe. [@ceremony-1979] [@schultz-lecture-1979] [@lewis-1954]

## In plain English

A farmer may continue using an old seed variety because a newer one requires credit, irrigation or reliable access to fertilizer that the farmer does not have. Calling the decision resistant to progress would miss the constraints. Schultz urged economists to understand poor people's opportunities and incentives, and to take investment in knowledge seriously. His lecture explicitly argues that poor people care about improving their own and their children's lives. [@schultz-lecture-1979]

Lewis asks how an expanding sector can turn available labor into sustained growth. If additional workers can leave some activities without a large loss of output, and new enterprises have productive capital and demand, they may be employed at substantially higher productivity. Reinvesting profits can then expand employment further.

The word **if** matters. People need usable opportunities, not merely a change of address. Lewis explicitly limits his surplus-labor assumption to economies where it fits; he does not claim that every country or every agricultural task has redundant labor. [@lewis-1954]

## The problem before this work

Treating development as the accumulation of machines alone overlooks the knowledge required to use them, the health needed to work and the incentives needed to adopt better techniques. Likewise, assuming that traditional agriculture is uniformly wasteful can confuse low productivity with inefficient choices among the options actually available.

The academy connects Schultz's agricultural research with his broader work on human capital. Education and health can influence productive capacity, so some expenditures conventionally treated as current consumption can also have investment consequences. That analytical perspective is not a claim that the value of a person is their earnings. [@ceremony-1979]

Lewis identified a different mismatch: models built around scarce labor and a fixed allocation of resources may not explain economies where capital is scarce and large numbers of people work in low-productivity activities. His 1954 essay deliberately adapts a classical framework linking distribution, saving and expansion. It begins by specifying where the labor-supply assumption should and should not apply. [@lewis-1954]

## What the laureates contributed

**Schultz** studied agriculture and the economic importance of human capabilities. The academy highlights education, health and the measurement of investment in people. His lecture stresses the inadequacy of judging poor people's behavior without understanding their preferences and constraints, and argues for the productive role of knowledge. These claims are grounded in his research agenda, not a guarantee of a particular return from every school or clinic. [@ceremony-1979] [@schultz-lecture-1979]

**Lewis** developed the dual-sector growth framework and studied international development and terms of trade. In the model emphasized by the academy, the expanding capitalist sector draws labor from other activities, generates profits and finances further capital formation. His original essay defines sectors through the use of reproducible capital rather than a perfect equation of countryside with agriculture and cities with industry. It even discusses capital-intensive plantations. [@ceremony-1979] [@lewis-1954]

The distinction matters. A modern farm can belong to the capital-using sector; an urban casual occupation may not. This is a model of economic organization and accumulation, not just a geographic migration story.

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented enterprise with capital of 100 units. Suppose each job requires 10 units of capital and each worker produces output worth 20 per year, with a wage of 12. Ignore all other costs and depreciation. The enterprise employs ten workers, has revenue of 200, pays 120 in wages and earns 80 in profit.

If half the profit is invested, capital rises by 40. At the assumed capital requirement, that permits four more jobs. The mechanism can repeat if workers remain available at the stated wage, demand supports sales and the added capital remains equally productive. This is a deliberately simplified illustration, not an estimate from Lewis or a proof that profits are automatically reinvested locally.

For a Schultz-style investment question, suppose training costs 200 directly and requires giving up 300 of current earnings. It is expected to increase earnings by 300 at the end of each of the next two years. At a 10 percent discount rate, those benefits have a present value of approximately 520.66, slightly above the total cost of 500.

That financial calculation excludes nonmonetary benefits, risk and effects on others. Nor does an observed wage difference prove that training caused it: people who obtain training may differ in prior opportunities and skills.

## The core economics

A simplified accumulation equation is

$$
K_{t+1}=(1-\delta)K_t+s_\Pi\Pi_t.
$$

$K_t$ is productive capital, $\delta$ its depreciation rate, $\Pi_t$ profits and $s_\Pi$ the fraction invested. For the invented example, $\delta=0$ and $s_\Pi=0.5$, so $K_{t+1}=100+40=140$. This accounting relationship does not establish why investment occurs or which technology will be chosen.

The human-capital illustration uses

$$
\operatorname{NPV}=-C+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\Delta y_t}{(1+r)^t}.
$$

$C$ includes direct and opportunity costs; $\Delta y_t$ is the expected additional earnings in year $t$, $r$ the discount rate and $T$ the evaluation horizon. Here NPV is $-500+300/1.1+300/1.1^2$, approximately 20.66. These are original teaching equations rather than transcriptions of the laureates' models.

In Lewis's framework, expansion eventually changes character when labor can no longer be recruited on the earlier terms. That transition depends on the outside sector, productivity and population as well as capital accumulation. Unlimited supply describes a range of the model, not an inexhaustible population or a permanent natural wage. [@lewis-1954]

## What changed as a result

The research widened the analysis of development beyond the quantity of physical investment. People's capabilities, sectoral organization, agricultural opportunities and the distribution of income all enter the explanation of growth. The academy emphasized the laureates' historical awareness and practical interest in policy. [@ceremony-1979]

For the reader, the recurring question is what makes a proposed opportunity usable. A machine without skills, training without a job market or a new crop without reliable complementary inputs may not produce the expected gain. That is a reason to investigate constraints rather than assume that one visible investment is sufficient.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

The surplus-labor assumption needs evidence at relevant seasons and locations. Migration can reduce agricultural output or raise food prices; profits may be consumed, invested abroad or placed in capital-intensive activities that create few jobs. The simple example deliberately holds those possibilities aside.

Human-capital returns also require careful causal estimation and attention to credit constraints, quality and unequal access. Higher average earnings do not alone show what a particular intervention will achieve. Schultz's and Lewis's different assumptions should be compared empirically rather than blended into one universal model. Original-edition review and independent checking of this draft remain outstanding.

## Key takeaways

- Development involves people's capabilities as well as physical equipment.
- Low productivity does not establish that people make irrational choices.
- Lewis's sectors are not perfectly identical to agriculture and industry.
- Reinvestment and usable employment opportunities are assumptions to investigate.
- Private earnings, causal effects and social benefits are different objects.
