## Abstract

Bertil Ohlin and James E. Meade shared the prize for contributions to international trade and international capital movements. Ohlin developed an account of trade based on countries' different relative supplies of productive factors, extending ideas associated with Eli Heckscher. Meade analyzed trade policies, institutions and the interaction between a country's domestic economy and international payments. Their contributions illuminate two related questions: why trade occurs and how an open economy can pursue several policy objectives at once. Neither question can be answered simply by declaring a trade surplus good or imports bad. [@ceremony-1977]

## In plain English

Two countries can use the same production techniques and still gain from exchanging goods. One may have relatively more labor and the other relatively more land or equipment. Goods use those resources in different proportions. Trading goods can therefore indirectly exchange the services of resources that do not themselves cross a border.

Ohlin's approach explains how resource abundance can influence specialization and factor rewards. Its claims are conditional: demand, technology, trade costs and the range of goods produced matter. The academy also emphasized that trade affects distribution inside each country, not just a national total. [@ceremony-1977]

Meade asked what governments can do when goals conflict. Stimulating demand may support employment but increase imports. A change that improves international competitiveness may have other effects at home. The challenge is to combine instruments whose effects are sufficiently different, while recognizing that other countries are making decisions too. [@ceremony-1977]

## The problem before this work

Ricardo's comparative-advantage theory explained trade through differences in relative production costs associated with technology. The academy presents Ohlin's contribution as a major extension: trade can arise even with identical technology when relative factor supplies differ. It explicitly recognizes Heckscher's earlier influence. [@ceremony-1977]

This does not replace comparative advantage with an unrelated principle. It offers an explanation for relative costs and how trade changes the demand for productive resources. A country's abundance must be assessed relative to other factors and to the comparison country, rather than by its absolute size alone.

International policy posed another difficulty. A government might achieve a domestic target while destabilizing its payments position, or defend an exchange rate by suppressing employment. Meade's research made the interaction of targets and instruments explicit. His later lecture also revisits weaknesses in treating full employment and stable prices as one undifferentiated goal. [@ceremony-1977] [@meade-lecture-1977]

## What the laureates contributed

**Ohlin** developed the factor-proportions approach associated with the Heckscher–Ohlin model. Countries tend, under the model's conditions, to export goods using relatively abundant factors intensively. Trade changes demand for those factors and can therefore affect wages, rents and other returns. The academy uses this structure to discuss the relationship between goods trade and factor mobility. [@ceremony-1977]

**Meade** investigated tariffs, customs unions, free-trade arrangements and comparisons between imperfect situations. He also studied how employment and international-payment objectives could be pursued with combinations of policy instruments. The academy highlights his attention to monetary policy, exchange-rate arrangements and cross-country policy interactions. [@ceremony-1977]

These are distinct contributions within international economics, not one coauthored winning paper. Nor does the award establish that every trade agreement increases welfare or that any particular exchange-rate regime is universally best.

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented two-country world with identical technologies. One unit of clothing requires two labor units and one capital unit; one machine requires one labor unit and two capital units. Country A has 100 labor units and 60 capital units. Country B has 60 labor units and 100 capital units.

If both resources are fully used, A can produce 46⅔ clothing units and 6⅔ machines. B can produce the reverse. At an illustrative common price of one unit of money for either good, each country's output is worth 53⅓. If consumers in both spend half their income on each good, each consumes 26⅔ of each. A exports 20 clothing units and imports 20 machines; B does the opposite.

The arithmetic is internally consistent with positive factor prices and diversified production. It illustrates a possible trading equilibrium, not a prediction that real countries always export their labor-intensive goods. It assumes free trade, identical techniques, no transport costs and the stated preferences.

Now consider an unrelated, deliberately simplified policy illustration. A demand expansion alone raises an employment index but worsens an external-pressure index. A second instrument affects the two differently. Combining them can reach a target that neither reaches alone. This is the instrument-assignment logic relevant to Meade, not evidence that real exchange-rate changes always improve a trade balance.

## The core economics

For the production example, resource constraints in Country A are

$$
2C+M\leq100,\qquad C+2M\leq60,
$$

where $C$ is clothing output and $M$ machine output. Solving both as equalities gives $C=140/3$ and $M=20/3$. If each good's price is 1, competitive zero-profit conditions give

$$
2w+r=1,\qquad w+2r=1,
$$

so the labor reward $w$ and capital-service reward $r$ are both $1/3$. This equality follows from identical technology, both goods being produced and the specified prices. Factor-price equalization in this example is not a claim about actual international wage equality.

For the policy illustration, define arbitrary instrument changes $a$ and $b$ by

$$
E=a+b,\qquad B=-a+b.
$$

$E$ is an employment-target improvement and $B$ an external-pressure change, in chosen index units. A target $E=10$, $B=0$ requires $a=b=5$. Instrument $a$ alone cannot achieve both. The key mathematical condition is that the instruments have independent effects; if their effects were proportional, two labels would not provide two effective levers.

## The foundational works

The academy identifies Ohlin's *Interregional and International Trade* and Meade's volumes on *The Theory of International Economic Policy* as central works. Meade's own lecture identifies the two volumes as *The Balance of Payments* and *Trade and Welfare*, and explains his intellectual debt to Ohlin. Those associations have been read in authoritative accounts; full original editions and publication metadata still need dedicated verification. [@ceremony-1977] [@meade-lecture-1977]

Meade's 1977 lecture, *The Meaning of “Internal Balance”*, is particularly valuable for its self-criticism. He distinguishes external balance, full employment and price stability, and questions assumptions inherited from earlier analysis. It is retrospective primary testimony, not a substitute for the underlying books. The opening sections were consulted directly. [@meade-lecture-1977]

## What changed as a result

The factor-proportions framework connected international trade with domestic resource scarcity and distribution. Meade's framework connected international policy with the simultaneous management of domestic objectives. Together they made international economics more than an accounting exercise in exports minus imports. [@ceremony-1977]

“External balance” also needs interpretation. The balance-of-payments accounts balance by construction when all entries and errors are included. The policy problem concerns a sustainable payments and financing position, reserve pressures or other specified objectives; it is not a plan to make an accounting identity start working.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

Identical technologies, well-functioning factor markets and frictionless goods exchange are strong assumptions. Different skills, production networks, trade barriers and incomplete specialization can change factor-price results. Gains to a country do not ensure compensation for people who lose.

Instrument assignment is similarly conditional. A depreciation need not immediately improve external payments, and other countries can respond. Meade's lecture explicitly reexamines the relation between full employment and price stability instead of assuming they coincide. Original-book consultation, fuller treatment of alternative trade explanations and independent review remain outstanding. [@meade-lecture-1977]

## Key takeaways

- Relative factor abundance can generate trade even with identical technology.
- Trade affects the rewards to resources and the distribution of income.
- Several objectives generally require instruments with sufficiently different effects.
- International policies interact across borders.
- Accounting balance and a sustainable external position are different ideas.
