## Abstract

George J. Stigler's research connected market theory with the costs and institutions that shape actual exchange. Buyers must search, firms must adapt, cartels must monitor members, and interested groups can influence regulation. The academy emphasized his studies of information, industrial organization and the causes and effects of regulatory legislation. A common thread is to explain behavior using the constraints people actually face rather than assuming that information and adjustment are free. The resulting account does not establish that every price difference is efficient or that all regulation serves producers. Those remain questions for evidence. [@ceremony-1982]

## In plain English

A shopper sees the same appliance offered at different prices. Why does the expensive shop have any customers? One possibility is that discovering the cheaper offer takes time. A busy shopper might sensibly stop searching before finding the lowest price.

The shop may also offer a different warranty or location, so not every observed price gap compares identical products. The point of an information-cost approach is to ask which differences and costs matter. It makes search part of the economic choice rather than treating incomplete knowledge as an unexplained error.

Stigler applied a similar habit of inquiry to institutions. Regulation is not simply an outside command imposed on an otherwise economic system. People and firms have reasons to seek particular rules, organize politically and influence implementation. Explaining the resulting policy requires studying those incentives as well as the policy's stated public purpose. [@ceremony-1982]

## The problem before this work

A fully informed competitive benchmark predicts outcomes under demanding conditions. Persistent price differences or slow movements between industries may seem inconsistent with it if obtaining information and adjusting are assumed costless. Once those activities use resources, the benchmark needs extension before it can explain the observation.

The presentation speech describes Stigler's program as building bridges between theory and facts. It also discusses cartel enforcement: firms seeking a common high price have incentives to undercut it, and monitoring is costly. Merely recognizing that firms would like monopoly profits does not show that they can sustain a cartel. [@ceremony-1982]

The regulation question requires a comparable distinction between intention and mechanism. A law described as protecting consumers may have effects that benefit incumbent producers. That observation could arise through capture, implementation difficulties, a genuine trade-off or several mechanisms at once. A useful theory must identify evidence that distinguishes them.

## What the laureates contributed

**Stigler was the sole recipient.** The academy highlighted his work on information acquisition and dissemination, adjustment across industries, market structure and regulation. It describes incomplete information as a consequence of costly search rather than simply a departure to be assumed away. [@ceremony-1982]

His research on regulatory processes examined cases in which organized producer interests influenced legislation and its implementation. The academy explicitly says the extent of the hypothesis's validity remained unknown. That qualification matters: the recognized contribution was a productive research approach, not proof that every regulator is captured. [@ceremony-1982]

The speech also credits empirical construction and testing. A theoretical explanation that search can produce dispersion does not establish how much dispersion it explains in a given market. Likewise, a political-incentive model needs observations about organization, rules and outcomes before it can diagnose a particular institution.

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented shopper who has found a price of 100 for an identical product. Looking at one more shop costs 3 units of time valued in money. Suppose, purely for this illustration, the next quote is equally likely to be 90 or 110. The shopper can keep the earlier offer if the new quote is worse.

The expected saving from one more search is therefore $0.5(100-90)+0.5(0)=5$. Subtracting the cost gives an expected net benefit of 2. Under these assumptions and with no other constraints, searching once more is worthwhile.

If the current offer were 92, the same possible next quotes would give an expected saving of only $0.5(92-90)=1$. That is below the search cost. The shopper would stop, even though a lower price might exist.

These are original teaching numbers, not estimates from Stigler. The result depends on beliefs about other quotes, whether offers remain available and how search costs are valued. It shows that a sensible stopping rule can leave buyers paying different prices; it does not demonstrate that the observed distribution in any actual market has that explanation.

## The core economics

Let $p$ be the best price found so far, $P$ the random price in one additional search and $c$ its cost. If the previous offer remains available, the immediate expected benefit is

$$
B(p)=\operatorname{E}\bigl[\max(p-P,0)\bigr].
$$

A one-step comparison favors another search when

$$
B(p)>c.
$$

The maximum prevents a worse quote from counting as a loss when the shopper can return to the earlier offer. If quotes expire, travel is irreversible or quality differs, the formulation must change. Repeated optimal search also requires a continuation-value calculation; this one-step illustration is not presented as the general solution.

For regulation, the comparable reasoning is qualitative here. A small group receiving a large benefit per member may find organization worthwhile, while a large group losing a small amount each may not. That asymmetry can influence policy, but it does not mechanically determine the outcome. Voting rules, institutions, countervailing organizations and public objectives matter. The academy's discussion motivates investigating the mechanism without assuming universal capture. [@ceremony-1982]

## What changed as a result

Stigler helped make information and the formation of regulation objects of economic analysis. The academy identifies the resulting research fields and emphasizes how costs of search, monitoring and adjustment can reconcile theory with observations that a simpler model misses. [@ceremony-1982]

For a reader, the practical questions become more precise. What does the decision maker know? What would learning more cost? Who gains from a rule, who bears the cost and how are those groups organized? Answering those questions is more informative than inferring motives solely from a policy's name.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

An information-cost explanation can be stretched too far if unobserved costs are chosen after seeing the outcome. Price dispersion may also reflect quality, discrimination, market power or temporary shocks. Empirical work must test alternatives rather than declare every unexplained gap rational.

Similarly, public-interest regulation and capture are not mutually exclusive in every case. A rule can address a real harm and still be shaped to favor incumbents. The academy's stated uncertainty about the reach of the capture hypothesis remains a useful caution. This draft needs original-article consultation, fuller empirical examples and independent review. [@ceremony-1982]

## Key takeaways

- Information and adjustment require resources.
- Searching less than exhaustively can be a reasoned choice.
- Cartels face monitoring and enforcement problems.
- Regulation's formation as well as its effects can be studied economically.
- A possible mechanism is a hypothesis to test, not a universal diagnosis.
