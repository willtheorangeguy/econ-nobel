## Abstract

Finn E. Kydland and Edward C. Prescott shared the prize for contributions to dynamic macroeconomics concerning policy consistency and business-cycle forces. A policy desirable before people make decisions may cease to be attractive after those decisions are fixed. Anticipating that incentive can undermine the original announcement. Their separate business-cycle work built quantitative models from forward-looking household and firm decisions and examined real supply disturbances. These contributions changed research methods and institutional questions. They do not establish that commitment is always superior or that every observed recession is an efficient response to technology. [@ceremony-2004]

## In plain English

A government promises how it will act, and people make investments or contracts in reliance on that promise. Later, when those decisions cannot easily be changed, the government may want a different action. People who anticipate that temptation will not necessarily behave as the original announcement hoped.

The difficulty does not require dishonest officials or changing objectives. The decision situation itself changes after private actions are taken. A rule or commitment mechanism can influence behavior before the later policy choice arises.

The other research theme asks how an economy responds over time to changes in productive conditions. An investment takes resources today and affects output later; a technology change can alter wages, work and saving decisions together. A dynamic model links those responses rather than treating each as an unrelated aggregate correlation. The academy connects both themes to the laureates' joint work. [@ceremony-2004]

## The problem before this work

Policy evaluation can assume that an announced plan will be carried out because it was initially optimal. That overlooks the incentive to revise the plan after expectations or investments have adjusted. A plan can be optimal as a commitment and fail to be optimal when reconsidered later.

Macroeconomic models also needed a more explicit account of expectations and decisions across time. Kydland and Prescott's quantitative approach derived aggregate movements from household and firm behavior under specified constraints, then used calibrated parameters and simulation to examine their implications. [@ceremony-2004]

The academy's historical summary describes a contrast with earlier models, but that contrast should not be exaggerated into a claim that all earlier economists believed stabilization was effortless. Nor do microeconomic foundations alone guarantee robustness. A model can specify decisions precisely and still omit institutions or mechanisms essential to an actual episode.

## What the laureates contributed

**Kydland and Prescott jointly** developed the policy-consistency analysis highlighted by the academy in their 1977 paper. The contribution directs attention from isolated policy actions to the incentives and institutions governing future decisions. Credibility becomes an outcome to explain rather than an adjective attached to an announcement. [@ceremony-2004]

Their **joint 1982 business-cycle paper** developed a quantitative dynamic equilibrium approach with real disturbances and time-consuming investment. The academy emphasizes model calibration and simulated time series as research tools. The method asks whether a specified mechanism can reproduce relevant features of observed fluctuations. [@ceremony-2004]

The work generated a broader program in which researchers could modify assumptions and compare implications. An influential benchmark need not be a complete account of the economy to organize useful questions. Its empirical claims still require evaluation against competing mechanisms and data.

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented policy game. Inflation is $\pi$, expected inflation $\pi^e$, and the output gap is $y=\pi-\pi^e$. The authority wants inflation near zero but output one unit above its benchmark, minimizing $L=\pi^2+(y-1)^2$.

If expectations are fixed at zero, choosing inflation of one half gives output of one half and loss $0.25+0.25=0.5$. Keeping the initial zero-inflation promise gives output zero and loss 1. The authority therefore has an incentive to depart from the promise after expectations are set.

If people anticipate the discretionary choice, expectations must be consistent with it. The equilibrium has expected and actual inflation both equal to 1, output zero and loss 2. A credible commitment to zero instead gives output zero and loss 1. The attempted output gain disappears, but inflation remains.

These are original teaching assumptions and numbers, not a reconstruction or estimate from the 1977 paper. The example shows how unchanged objectives can generate a commitment problem. It does not prove that a zero-inflation rule is optimal under supply shocks, nominal rigidities or other omitted conditions.

## The core economics

For the teaching game, substituting the output relation into the loss gives

$$
L=\pi^2+(\pi-\pi^e-1)^2.
$$

Holding expectations fixed, minimization yields

$$
\pi=\frac{1+\pi^e}{2}.
$$

Consistency requires $\pi=\pi^e$, giving $\pi=1$. The distinction is between optimizing after expectations are fixed and choosing a rule before they are formed. A change in preferences is not needed for the two choices to differ.

For the business-cycle theme, an illustrative production relationship is

$$
Y_t=A_tK_t^\theta L_t^{1-\theta},\qquad0<\theta<1.
$$

$Y_t$ is output, $K_t$ capital, $L_t$ labor input and $A_t$ productivity. Holding inputs fixed, a ten-percent fall in $A_t$ lowers output ten percent. A full dynamic model does not hold everything else fixed: wages, work, consumption and investment respond under its assumptions.

That production identity alone is not the 1982 model and does not establish that observed output movements are caused mainly by technology. Calibration assigns parameters using stated evidence and targets; simulation then explores the consequences. Matching selected moments is informative but cannot by itself identify the unique true mechanism.

## What changed as a result

The policy work made institutional credibility central to macroeconomics. The cycle work helped establish calibrated dynamic modeling and simulation as major research practices. The academy describes influence on both subsequent research and policy institutions. [@ceremony-2004]

For readers, the useful questions are when decisions are made, which promises can be changed and what agents anticipate. For a quantitative model, ask which parameters are measured independently, which outcomes were targeted and which predictions genuinely test the mechanism.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

A commitment can be harmful if it binds an unsuitable policy in unforeseen conditions. The relevant comparison includes contingent rules, accountability and implementation, not only rigid rules versus unconstrained discretion.

Real-business-cycle mechanisms also face questions about the interpretation of productivity, labor-market behavior and demand disturbances. An equilibrium model does not make every fluctuation socially desirable. The academy's broad statements about explanatory success do not replace independent empirical evaluation. This draft needs original-paper reading, fuller discussion of competing models and independent review before it is complete.

## Key takeaways

- A plan can lose credibility because incentives change after private decisions.
- Time inconsistency does not require changing preferences or bad intentions.
- Commitment results depend on the environment and available institutions.
- Dynamic models link decisions and constraints across time.
- Calibration and fit are evidence to assess, not proof of a unique mechanism.
