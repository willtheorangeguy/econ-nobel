## Abstract

Peter A. Diamond, Dale T. Mortensen and Christopher A. Pissarides shared the prize for analysis of markets with search frictions. Workers and employers do not find suitable matches instantaneously, and the terms of a match affect both search and vacancy creation. The resulting equilibrium can include unemployment and unfilled jobs at the same time. The framework links individual decisions with aggregate flows and external effects. An equilibrium need not be efficient, and a policy conclusion requires more than observing one unemployment rate. [@ceremony-2010] [@academy-2010]

## In plain English

An unemployed electrician and a firm advertising for a nurse do not form a useful match merely because one needs work and the other needs a worker. Even suitable partners must locate each other, assess qualifications and agree on terms. Time and information are part of the transaction.

Employers also choose whether to post vacancies, while workers choose how and where to search. Those choices influence the opportunities available to others. A job seeker's additional effort can help a firm recruit but make another seeker's search more difficult.

Search-and-matching theory puts these processes inside the economic model. The academy emphasizes applications to labor markets while noting that housing and other markets also involve costly partner search. A count of unsatisfied buyers or sellers is therefore not enough to infer how prices, wages or quantities should adjust. [@ceremony-2010]

## The problem before this work

A frictionless market clears through a price, with partners immediately available. That benchmark cannot by itself explain the time required to fill a vacancy or the simultaneous presence of unemployed people and job openings.

Earlier search models studied individuals deciding whether to accept an offer. An aggregate labor-market theory also needs employers' recruitment decisions, wage determination and the creation and destruction of jobs. The official background describes how the laureates developed the theory toward that broader system. [@academy-2010]

Pissarides's lecture distinguishes looking for an acceptable wage from two-sided search for a suitable match. Jobs and workers differ, so the firm is not merely waiting passively for someone to accept its posted wage. The matching process itself can take time even when both sides would benefit from agreement. [@pissarides-lecture-2010]

## What the laureates contributed

**Diamond** developed fundamental analysis of search markets, including how small search costs can have large effects on price outcomes under particular assumptions. The official background identifies his 1971 price-adjustment paper and later contributions to equilibrium and external effects. [@academy-2010]

**Mortensen** developed labor-market search analysis, including work on unemployment dynamics and job flows. **Pissarides** developed equilibrium matching analysis connecting workers' and firms' searches with recruitment and wage decisions. Their joint research on job creation and destruction is one verified bibliographic entry into the shared program. The contributions overlap rather than divide neatly into three isolated models. [@academy-2010] [@pissarides-lecture-2010] [@mortensen-pissarides-1994]

The framework is often called the Diamond–Mortensen–Pissarides model. Pissarides's retrospective also credits earlier search researchers and describes the matching function as an aggregate summary of frictions rather than a complete account of every search action. [@pissarides-lecture-2010]

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented two-state labor market with a constant workforce. Employed workers lose jobs at a flow rate of 0.02 per month. Unemployed workers find jobs at a flow rate of 0.18 per month. Treat the rates as continuous-time intensities, not exact monthly transition probabilities.

In steady state, flows into and out of unemployment balance. The unemployment share is $0.02/(0.02+0.18)=0.10$, or 10 percent. That stock can remain constant while many individual people move between employment and unemployment.

If job finding rises to 0.38 while separation stays unchanged, the steady-state share becomes $0.02/(0.02+0.38)=0.05$, or 5 percent. A lower separation rate could also reduce unemployment. The same observed stock can therefore reflect different underlying flows.

The numbers are original teaching assumptions, not estimates from the laureates. A policy cannot simply choose the job-finding rate as an independent lever: it depends on vacancies, matching, worker and firm behavior, and other conditions. The flow calculation shows what must be explained, not how to achieve the change.

## The core economics

Let $u$ be the unemployed share, $s$ the separation rate and $f$ the job-finding rate. A simple flow equation is

$$
\dot u=s(1-u)-fu.
$$

The dot denotes change per unit of time. With constant rates, the steady state is

$$
u^*=\frac{s}{s+f}.
$$

This is a balance of flows, not a statement that the same people remain unemployed forever.

A teaching matching function is

$$
m=\mu U^\alpha V^{1-\alpha},\qquad0<\alpha<1.
$$

$U$ is the number searching, $V$ vacancies, $m$ matches per unit of time and $\mu$ a matching-efficiency parameter with appropriate units. The rate of finding work is $m/U$; the rate of filling a vacancy is $m/V$.

More vacancies relative to searchers can improve workers' prospects while making each vacancy harder to fill. Recruitment and search therefore affect other participants' opportunities. A full model also determines wages, entry and job destruction. The equations here are explanatory summaries, not a reproduction of the complete 1994 model.

A fall in unemployment caused by fewer separations can have different implications from one caused by faster matching. Tracking both flows helps distinguish mechanisms that a single headline unemployment figure cannot reveal.

## What changed as a result

The research made unemployment, vacancies and wages joint outcomes of a market with costly matching. The academy describes its influence on labor-market policy analysis, including insurance, employment services and hiring or separation rules. [@ceremony-2010]

It also changes the interpretation of equilibrium. A model can be internally consistent while leaving valuable matches unrealized. External effects mean privately chosen search or vacancy creation need not maximize social gains. That creates a policy question, not an automatic endorsement of every proposed intervention.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

An aggregate matching function compresses many differences among people, occupations and places. Its efficiency parameter can conceal changing composition or institutions. Wage bargaining, recruitment costs and productivity assumptions can also change quantitative predictions.

Unemployment insurance combines income protection with incentives and possible effects on match quality. A conclusion based only on search duration omits part of the welfare problem. Likewise, describing unemployment as an equilibrium does not label it voluntary or desirable. This draft needs original-paper consultation, fuller empirical assessment and independent review before it is complete.

## Key takeaways

- Finding a suitable partner takes time and resources.
- Unemployment and vacancies can coexist in equilibrium.
- Stocks must be explained through job-finding and separation flows.
- Search and recruitment create effects on other participants.
- Policy evaluation needs welfare, matching quality and behavioral responses together.
