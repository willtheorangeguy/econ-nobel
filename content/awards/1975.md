## Abstract

Leonid Kantorovich and Tjalling C. Koopmans shared the prize for the theory of optimal resource allocation. Their work asks how activities can be combined when labor, machines, materials or time are limited. Linear programming identifies the best feasible plan for a stated objective and, through its dual problem, values the scarce resources supporting that plan. Those shadow prices reveal opportunity costs within the model. They do not determine a morally correct objective, guarantee truthful reporting or solve every practical problem of economic organization. The academy connected the laureates' work across different political and institutional settings. [@ceremony-1975]

## In plain English

A workshop can produce chairs or tables. Both need cutting time and finishing time, but in different proportions. Making the product with the highest price is not necessarily best: it might consume the resource that is most difficult to obtain, crowding out a more valuable combination.

The problem is to evaluate the whole feasible plan. A resource's importance depends on what additional output it permits at the margin. An unused hour on a machine that is already idle may be worth nothing in the current plan; an extra hour on the bottleneck machine may be valuable.

Kantorovich and Koopmans helped connect these practical scheduling questions with economic theory. Prices appear not only as numbers handed to a firm from a market, but also as accounting valuations implied by an optimization problem. The academy stresses the possibility of using those valuations to coordinate decisions across units. [@ceremony-1975]

## The problem before this work

Resource constraints interact. Solving each department's problem separately can leave one resource exhausted and another stranded. Meanwhile, a plan can be technically feasible without being efficient: another combination may meet the same requirements using fewer scarce inputs.

Koopmans's lecture begins with production choices at the plant level and explains why alternative techniques create a genuine optimization problem. If there were only one feasible recipe for a required output, there would be little to choose. When several activities can substitute for one another, the mix matters. [@koopmans-lecture-1975]

There was also a computational problem. A practical model can contain many activities and resource restrictions. A useful theory must help characterize a solution and guide its calculation. Historical credit extends beyond the two recipients: Koopmans's own retrospective identifies George Dantzig and other researchers in the independent development of linear programming. [@koopmans-lecture-1975]

## What the laureates contributed

**Kantorovich** developed an optimization approach to production planning beginning in the late 1930s. His work linked optimal plans with valuations for resources, initially described as resolving multipliers. The academy emphasized both the mathematical method and its implications for decentralized decisions in planning. [@ceremony-1975]

**Koopmans** worked on transportation and activity analysis, connecting production efficiency, resource allocation and competitive prices. The academy also recognized his research on optimal growth and the division of resources between present consumption and investment for the future. These intertemporal choices involve judgments about different generations as well as technical constraints. [@ceremony-1975]

Their shared result was not that all economies should have the same institutions. Similar optimization mathematics can be applied within very different settings. Whether the data are credible, decisions can be implemented and incentives support the plan remains a separate question. Kantorovich's lecture discusses precisely those obstacles. [@kantorovich-lecture-1975]

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented workshop making divisible quantities of chairs $c$ and tables $t$. A chair uses two hours of cutting and one of finishing. A table uses one hour of cutting and two of finishing. The workshop has 100 cutting hours and 80 finishing hours. Net contributions before charging these fixed-capacity resources are 40 units per chair and 50 per table.

A plan of 40 chairs and 20 tables uses all capacity: $2(40)+20=100$ cutting hours and $40+2(20)=80$ finishing hours. Its objective value is $40(40)+50(20)=2{,}600$.

Now value cutting capacity at 10 units per hour and finishing at 20. The resource cost assigned to a chair is $2(10)+20=40$; for a table it is $10+2(20)=50$. The total value of available resources is $100(10)+80(20)=2{,}600$. Since every product's contribution is no greater than its assigned resource cost, no feasible combination can beat that total. Our plan reaches it, so it is optimal.

Those numbers also explain scarcity. A small increase in cutting capacity is worth 10 per hour at this solution; finishing is worth 20. The rates apply while the same local constraints and activities determine the optimum. They are not permanent market prices, and integer-only production would require a different model.

## The core economics

A standard linear program is

$$
\max_{x\geq0} c^\top x \quad\text{subject to}\quad Ax\leq b.
$$

Here $x$ lists activity levels, $c$ their objective contributions, $A$ the resource requirements and $b$ available resources. The superscript $\top$ denotes a transpose, so $c^\top x$ adds up contributions.

Its dual problem is

$$
\min_{\lambda\geq0} b^\top\lambda
\quad\text{subject to}\quad A^\top\lambda\geq c.
$$

The vector $\lambda$ contains resource valuations. Every dual-feasible valuation gives an upper bound on every primal-feasible production plan. Under the standard conditions for a finite attainable optimum, strong duality makes the best values equal. In the example, the feasible plan and valuation both reach 2,600, proving optimality directly.

A resource can be abundant at the margin even if it is physically limited. Complementary slackness implies a positive valuation can attach only to a binding resource constraint in this formulation. A binding constraint can nevertheless have a zero valuation in degenerate cases; “fully used” does not automatically mean “valuable to expand.” These equations are an original teaching presentation of the optimization logic.

## What changed as a result

Optimization made opportunity cost operational. A plan can be assessed against explicit alternatives, and the value of relaxing a constraint can be calculated instead of guessed. The theory connects local choices to system-wide scarcity through supporting valuations. [@ceremony-1975]

It also clarifies what efficiency cannot decide. Maximizing output, profit or a specified social objective can produce different plans. A mathematically optimal plan is optimal for the problem posed. Koopmans opens his lecture by emphasizing the need to define what counts as resources, use and best; those definitions change with the application. [@koopmans-lecture-1975]

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

Linear models assume proportional input requirements and divisible activities. Fixed setup costs, indivisible projects, uncertainty and strategic behavior may demand other formulations. Shadow prices can change abruptly when a different constraint becomes decisive.

Kantorovich explicitly discusses data errors, the difficulty of forecasting, differences between theoretical valuations and actual incentives, and the challenge of specifying consumer objectives. Optimization does not eliminate those problems; it makes some of their implications inspectable. A complete editorial treatment still requires primary-publication review and external checking of the historical and mathematical account. [@kantorovich-lecture-1975]

## Key takeaways

- Efficiency concerns the best feasible combination of activities for a stated objective.
- Shadow prices measure model-specific marginal scarcity.
- Primal and dual solutions can jointly demonstrate optimality.
- Technical efficiency does not settle distributional or intergenerational values.
- Reliable data and incentives remain necessary for a calculated plan to be useful.
