## Abstract

Robert C. Merton and Myron S. Scholes shared the prize for a new method of determining derivative values. Fischer Black's collaboration is inseparable from the foundational research, although he was not a recipient. The method constructs a trading position that reproduces or hedges the derivative's payoff under specified market and asset-dynamics assumptions. No-arbitrage then links the derivative's price to the cost of that position. This avoids assigning the option an independent, arbitrary risk premium. It does not mean that options are riskless, that volatility is irrelevant or that a hedge works perfectly in actual markets. [@ceremony-1997]

## In plain English

A call option gives its holder the right, without an obligation, to buy an asset at a specified price. Its value depends on what the asset might be worth when that right can be exercised. The right is valuable because the holder can use it in favorable states and decline in unfavorable ones.

The pricing challenge is not solved just by averaging possible payoffs. Risky payoffs generally require an account of how they are valued. The research recognized by the prize supplied a different route: identify trades in the underlying asset and borrowing or lending that produce the same payoff.

If two positions reliably deliver the same future resources under the model, different prices would create an arbitrage opportunity. The replicating position therefore anchors the derivative's value. The academy emphasizes that the method, more than one formula alone, opened a broad approach to valuation. [@ceremony-1997]

## The problem before this work

An option's exposure to the underlying asset changes with the asset's price and with time. Discounting its expected payoff using a fixed risk premium chosen by analogy with an ordinary share can therefore miss the relevant changing risk.

The academy describes the development of the pricing research around 1970 and the 1973 Black–Scholes publication, with Merton's influence and extensions. Earlier option trading existed, but a general and workable valuation method remained a major obstacle. [@ceremony-1997]

There is an important interpretation issue in the speech's striking phrase about using no risk premium. It does not say investors require no compensation for risk in the world. The model derives the option price from hedging and no-arbitrage, so one need not separately specify the option's expected-return premium. The risks and market assumptions enter the construction itself.

## What the laureates contributed

**Scholes**, with **Black**, developed the option-pricing formulation published in 1973. **Merton** contributed to the method's development and generalized it. The academy explicitly acknowledges all three and notes that Black had died before the 1997 award. The historical explanation must retain his authorship even though the prize went to the two living recipients. [@ceremony-1997]

The central advance links dynamic trading, hedging and valuation. In a suitable continuous-time model, a changing position in the underlying asset and a risk-free asset can locally remove the relevant source of uncertainty. The resulting restrictions determine a price under the model's conditions.

The academy also describes applications to corporate liabilities, guarantees and the value of flexibility in real investment. Such extensions need their own assumptions about tradability and hedging. A real project is not automatically priced exactly like an exchange-traded option merely because it includes a choice. [@ceremony-1997]

## A concrete worked example

Consider a deliberately simple one-period teaching model. A share costs 100 today and will be worth either 120 or 80 at the end. The interest rate is zero. A call option with strike 100 pays 20 in the high state and zero in the low state.

Buy half a share and borrow 40. In the high state the share position is worth 60; repaying 40 leaves 20. In the low state it is worth 40; repaying 40 leaves zero. The position exactly reproduces the call's two possible payoffs.

Its initial cost is $0.5(100)-40=10$. Under the model's no-arbitrage assumptions, the option must also cost 10. A different price would allow equivalent payoff streams to be exchanged at different costs.

No actual probability for the high state was needed for that replication calculation. That does not imply that probabilities never matter in finance. The asset price and available trading opportunities already embody valuation information. This two-state example illustrates the logic; it is not the original continuous-time derivation or a real-market price recommendation.

## The core economics

The call payoff at expiry is

$$
C_T=\max(S_T-K,0),
$$

where $S_T$ is the asset price at expiry, $K$ the strike and $C_T$ the payoff. In the teaching model, let $\Delta$ be shares held and $B$ the risk-free position, with borrowing represented by a negative number. Replication requires

$$
120\Delta+B=20,\qquad80\Delta+B=0.
$$

Subtracting gives $\Delta=0.5$, then $B=-40$. The current option value is

$$
C_0=\Delta S_0+B=10.
$$

A risk-neutral representation of the same model uses a probability that makes the underlying asset's expected return equal to the risk-free rate. With zero interest here, that probability is one half. The discounted expected option payoff is then 10. This constructed probability is a valuation device, not necessarily the actual likelihood of the high state.

Continuous-time replication requires rebalancing rather than one fixed half-share position. Assumptions about continuous trading, price dynamics, volatility, financing and transaction costs therefore do substantive work. The price restriction is conditional on those assumptions, not a statement that maintaining a hedge in practice has no cost or risk.

## What changed as a result

The research made derivative valuation more systematic by connecting prices to replicating opportunities. The academy describes broad influence on financial markets and applications beyond them. The important intellectual shift is from guessing a derivative-specific discount rate to analyzing equivalent state-contingent resources and trading strategies. [@ceremony-1997]

It also clarifies why a formula is not the whole task. A user must specify the contract, the underlying dynamics and which risks can be hedged. Different exercise rights, dividends, jumps or financing constraints can require different models.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

Actual markets have transaction costs, discrete trading, changing volatility, liquidity constraints and jumps. A hedge designed for one stochastic process can fail when the process or market conditions differ. Model error and implementation risk remain even if the algebra is correct.

Some risks cannot be spanned by traded assets, so replication may not determine a unique price without additional assumptions. The method's power should not be confused with universal completeness. This draft requires original-article reading, fuller empirical discussion and independent review before it becomes a finished scholarly account.

## Key takeaways

- An option's payoff is asymmetric across future states.
- Replication can link its price to existing asset trades.
- No-arbitrage avoids independently guessing an option risk premium.
- Risk-neutral probabilities are valuation tools, not necessarily actual probabilities.
- Hedging results depend on market, trading and price-process assumptions.
