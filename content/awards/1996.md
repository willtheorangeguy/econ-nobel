## Abstract

James A. Mirrlees and William Vickrey shared the prize for fundamental contributions to incentives under asymmetric information. A government, seller or contracting party may need information held by someone whose interests differ from its own. Asking for that information does not ensure a truthful answer. The rules determine what people have reason to reveal and do. Vickrey's auction analysis and Mirrlees's taxation and contract work made those incentive constraints part of the design problem. The result is a framework for conditional institutional comparisons, not a guarantee that one mechanism works in every setting. [@ceremony-1996]

## In plain English

A seller wants to allocate an item to the buyer who values it most, but cannot directly observe each buyer's valuation. If the winner pays their own bid, bidding less than the true value may be attractive. Changing the payment rule can change that incentive.

A tax authority faces a related but different problem. It sees earnings more readily than a person's productivity, effort or opportunities. A tax system that redistributes income also changes which earning choices people find worthwhile. The authority cannot evaluate redistribution while assuming everyone continues earning exactly as before.

The common idea is to work with the information and behavior the institution can actually obtain. Incentives are not an inconvenient complication added after the ideal allocation is chosen. They help determine which allocations are feasible. The academy connects the laureates' work across auctions, insurance, taxation and principal-agent problems. [@ceremony-1996]

## The problem before this work

A planner with complete information might choose transfers or instructions based directly on private characteristics. Real institutions often cannot verify those characteristics. A high-productivity person may choose an earning pattern that resembles a lower-productivity person if doing so brings favorable treatment.

Likewise, a manager may know more than shareholders about effort or operating conditions. A contract based on output must distinguish, imperfectly, between actions and circumstances. Giving strong incentives can expose an agent to risk; providing complete insurance can weaken some incentives. The appropriate contract depends on what is observable and on the parties' objectives.

The academy describes Vickrey's earlier formulation of the income-tax problem and Mirrlees's later solution approach. That history is important: the shared award reflects connected contributions and a general method, rather than two unrelated applications accidentally using the same vocabulary. [@ceremony-1996]

## What the laureates contributed

**Vickrey** showed how a second-price sealed-bid auction can make truthful bidding a weakly dominant strategy in the standard private-value setting. The highest bidder wins but pays the highest competing bid, so the bid determines whether the buyer wins rather than directly setting the price paid. The conditions behind that statement matter. [@ceremony-1996]

**Mirrlees** developed the analysis of optimal income taxation with private information and endogenous work choices. The academy also highlighted his contribution to principal-agent contracts, where actions or circumstances are not fully observable. His methods made incentive compatibility part of a broader constrained optimization problem. [@ceremony-1996]

The programs share an analytical discipline: choose rules while anticipating how people respond to them. They do not imply that every privately known characteristic should be revealed or that efficiency is the only relevant institutional objective.

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented bidder who values an item at 100 units. In a second-price auction, suppose the highest other bid is 80. Bidding 100 wins, requires payment of 80 and gives a surplus of 20. Bidding 90 has the same result; bidding 70 loses and gives zero.

Now suppose instead that the highest other bid is 120. A truthful bid of 100 loses, which is desirable because winning at a payment of 120 would give a loss of 20. Overbidding at 130 would create exactly that unwanted outcome.

The key comparison is to the highest competing bid, not to a price the bidder controls after winning. If that threshold is below the bidder's value, winning is beneficial; if above, losing is better. Bidding the value implements those choices without needing to know the threshold beforehand.

These are original teaching figures. They assume a single item, private values, enforceable payments and no relevant budget or externality complications. If winning conveys information about a common uncertain value, or if bidders coordinate, the same simple argument may not describe the problem. Truthful bidding in the standard mechanism is weakly, not always strictly, better than every alternative.

## The core economics

For a bidder with value $v$ facing highest competing bid $h$, payoff from winning is

$$
u=v-h,
$$

and payoff from losing is zero. A bid equal to $v$ wins precisely in the cases where the threshold is favorable, subject to the specified tie rule. If everyone follows that strategy under the standard assumptions, the highest-value bidder receives the item.

For taxation, let productivity be $w>0$, labor effort $\ell$, earnings $z=w\ell$, consumption $c$ and effort cost $v(\ell)$. A simple utility representation is $c-v(z/w)$. If the authority offers a menu indexed by a reported type, an incentive constraint requires the person's assigned option to be at least as attractive as any other option:

$$
c(w)-v\!\left(\frac{z(w)}{w}\right)
\geq c(\hat w)-v\!\left(\frac{z(\hat w)}{w}\right).
$$

$\hat w$ is a possible alternative report or menu selection; the person's actual productivity in both effort calculations remains $w$. This is teaching notation for the constraint, not a transcription of Mirrlees's model. A complete tax problem also needs a resource constraint, social objective, type distribution and participation assumptions. The inequality alone does not determine an optimal tax rate.

## What changed as a result

The research made informational constraints central to institutional design. A proposed rule could be evaluated not only for the allocation it requests but also for whether people have reason to comply with it. The academy describes the resulting methods as widely applicable across contracts and markets. [@ceremony-1996]

For a reader, the useful test is to ask what an informed participant would gain from selecting a different option or reporting a different value. A mechanism is not incentive compatible merely because its designer prefers honest responses.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

Mechanism conclusions depend on the information structure, feasible actions and enforcement. Quasilinear preferences and transferable money can simplify the analysis but exclude important distributional or credit constraints. A second-price auction's allocation result also does not by itself settle revenue, fairness or resistance to manipulation under other assumptions.

Taxation adds value judgments about redistribution and uncertainty about actual behavioral responses. A theoretically optimal schedule under one objective and distribution need not remain optimal under another. This draft needs full original-text consultation, broader practical evidence and independent review before it is complete.

## Key takeaways

- Private information changes what institutions can feasibly achieve.
- Rules must anticipate strategic responses.
- Second-price truthful bidding relies on a specified private-value setting.
- Incentive compatibility and resource feasibility are different constraints.
- An optimal mechanism is conditional on its objectives, information and enforcement.
