## Abstract

Ben S. Bernanke, Douglas W. Diamond and Philip H. Dybvig shared the prize for research on banks and financial crises. Diamond and Dybvig showed how intermediaries can reconcile savers' uncertain liquidity needs with productive long-term investment, while creating vulnerability to runs. Diamond also analyzed delegated monitoring of borrowers. Bernanke investigated how disrupted intermediation contributed to the depth and persistence of the Great Depression. The shared lesson is that banks provide economically valuable services whose breakdown can damage the wider economy. It is not a claim that every institution should receive an unconditional bailout. [@press-2022]

## In plain English

A saver may unexpectedly need money tomorrow. A business may need several years before its project produces enough to repay a loan. If every saver required the investment itself to be immediately reversible, useful long-term projects would be harder to finance.

A bank pools many savers. Normally, only some need early withdrawals, allowing the bank to provide access while keeping other resources invested. It also develops information about borrowers that individual savers may find costly to collect.

The same arrangement can be fragile. If depositors believe others will withdraw immediately, waiting may become risky even when the underlying investment is productive. A rush to withdraw can force liquidation and validate the fear. Separately, losses on bad assets can make an institution insolvent even without a purely self-fulfilling run. Those mechanisms must be distinguished. [@press-2022]

## The problem before this work

A view of banks as passive channels for money misses why their particular liabilities and information relationships matter. If anyone could replace a failed lender instantly at no cost, a bank collapse would have much smaller consequences for borrowers.

Liquidity also cannot be assessed by comparing final asset value with final promised payments alone. A bank may be able to repay if projects mature but unable to meet every claim immediately without costly disruption. The timing of demands changes feasibility.

Historical crises involve both financial quantities and relationships. Falling money supply can matter, but so can the cost of identifying creditworthy borrowers after an intermediary fails. Bernanke's contribution investigates this additional propagation mechanism; it should not be described as proving that monetary explanations are irrelevant. [@press-2022] [@bernanke-1983]

## What the laureates contributed

**Diamond and Dybvig** developed a model of liquidity provision with multiple possible outcomes, including a run. Their research explains why demandable claims can be useful despite their fragility and why credible institutional safeguards can change incentives to run. [@diamond-dybvig-1983] [@press-2022]

**Diamond** also analyzed delegated monitoring. Rather than every saver separately checking every borrower, an intermediary can perform monitoring on their behalf. The intermediary itself must have incentives to act properly, so delegation does not make the information problem vanish. [@diamond-1984]

**Bernanke** studied nonmonetary effects of financial disruption during the Great Depression. The academy emphasizes the loss of valuable borrower information and impaired credit allocation when banks fail. That historical mechanism complements rather than automatically replaces other explanations of the Depression. [@press-2022] [@bernanke-1983]

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented bank with 100 savers depositing one unit each. Each unit invested produces 1.5 at the final date or can be liquidated early for one. The deposit contract promises an early withdrawer 1.1, with remaining resources paid to those who wait.

If 20 savers need early money, the bank liquidates 22 units of investment to pay them. The remaining 78 produce 117 later, giving each of the 80 waiting savers $117/80=1.4625$. Under these assumptions the contract can serve the early group and preserve substantial long-term investment.

If all 100 seek early payment, their claims total 110 but immediate liquidation produces only 100. Those arriving late may receive less or nothing under sequential service. Expecting others to run can therefore create an incentive to join them.

This is an original feasibility illustration, not an optimal Diamond–Dybvig contract. Preferences, uncertain liquidity types and the exact service rules would be needed to establish optimality and equilibrium. It assumes no loss in the project's final productivity, illustrating how a timing problem can arise even without such a loss.

For monitoring, imagine 20 lenders each spending 2 to assess the same borrower. Total duplication costs 40. Delegating one assessment could save resources, but only if the assessment is reliable and the delegate's incentives are addressed. Cheap delegation is not automatically trustworthy delegation.

## The core economics

Let $n$ be the number of unit deposits, $w$ the number of early withdrawers, $c_1$ the early payment per person and $R$ the final return on an investment unit. If early liquidation returns one, remaining final resources are

$$
R(n-wc_1).
$$

When $w<n$, the feasible late payment is

$$
c_2=\frac{R(n-wc_1)}{n-w},
$$

provided the bank has enough resources for the early claims. In the example, $n=100$, $w=20$, $c_1=1.1$ and $R=1.5$.

A run changes $w$, so a contract feasible for normal liquidity needs may fail when everyone demands early payment. If $c_1>1$, immediate claims from all depositors exceed the proceeds from liquidating the entire investment.

These equations describe the teaching balance sheet only. They omit reserves, equity, uncertain asset returns, banking networks and central-bank facilities. A full theory must derive choices and beliefs rather than infer equilibrium from one accounting identity.

Deposit insurance or emergency liquidity can alter run incentives when credible and appropriately designed. Their availability also changes incentives for banks and creditors, so supervision, funding and loss allocation matter. A public guarantee cannot costlessly turn a fundamentally worthless asset into a productive one. [@press-2022]

## What changed as a result

The research made liquidity transformation and information production central to analysis of banking. It also showed why the consequences of a failure extend beyond losses to a bank's owners: borrowers can lose access to an intermediary that understood them. [@press-2022]

For readers, the distinction among illiquidity, insolvency and coordination failure is practical. They can interact, but a response aimed at one may not solve another. Policy evaluation must consider confidence, asset quality and the ability to restore credit relationships together.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

Real crises involve payment systems, wholesale funding, cross-border obligations and interconnected balance sheets that a small model omits. Runs can reflect genuine bad news as well as coordination, and distinguishing their roles requires evidence.

Guarantees can reduce panic while encouraging risk taking or shifting losses to the public. The appropriate response depends on institutional constraints and distribution as well as aggregate stability. This draft needs original-paper consultation, fuller policy analysis and independent review before it is complete.

## Key takeaways

- Banks provide liquidity while financing longer-term investment.
- A useful deposit contract can also create run vulnerability.
- Monitoring and borrower relationships have economic value.
- Illiquidity and insolvency are different problems that can interact.
- Safeguards must account for incentives and who bears losses.
