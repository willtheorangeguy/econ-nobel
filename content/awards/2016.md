## Abstract

Oliver Hart and Bengt Holmström shared the prize for contributions to contract theory. Holmström analyzed incentives when one party cannot fully observe another's actions, including how informative performance measures, risk and multiple tasks affect rewards. Hart developed incomplete-contract analysis in which ownership and decision rights matter because not every future action can be specified and enforced in advance. Both approaches explain how cooperation can be improved when interests differ. They do not imply that stronger performance pay or private ownership is universally better. [@ceremony-2016] [@press-2016]

## In plain English

A manager can work hard and still face weak sales because the market turns against the firm. Rewarding sales alone may expose the manager to risks unrelated to effort. Yet paying exactly the same amount regardless of behavior can weaken incentives.

Measurement can also distort priorities. If a school rewards only a narrow test score, staff may concentrate on the tested activity and neglect valuable work that the score misses. More forceful rewards are not automatically better when the target is incomplete.

Some problems cannot be resolved by adding another payment clause. A relationship may face future circumstances that are too difficult to describe or verify in court. Then it matters who has authority to decide what happens. Hart's approach makes those rights an economic choice rather than treating ownership as an empty legal label. [@press-2016]

## The problem before this work

A complete contingent agreement would specify every relevant action and payment for every possible situation. Real contracts cannot always do that. Actions can be hidden, outcomes noisy, and some events observable to the parties but difficult for an outside enforcer to verify.

An incentive contract must therefore work through available signals. Its design changes both effort and risk allocation. If several tasks matter but only one is measurable, a reward aimed at that measure can alter the balance of activities.

Incomplete contracts pose a different problem. Parties may make investments before they know exactly how cooperation will unfold. Later bargaining determines how gains are divided, and anticipated control rights can affect willingness to invest today. The best allocation of authority depends on whose investments and outside options matter, not simply who currently operates an asset.

## What the laureates contributed

**Holmström** developed principal–agent analysis, including the informativeness principle: compensation should reflect information relevant to assessing the agent's action under the model's conditions. The academy also highlights work on career concerns, teams and multiple tasks. These contributions extend beyond a simple formula tying pay to output. [@press-2016] [@holmstrom-1979]

**Hart** developed the incomplete-contract approach to ownership and control, including influential joint research with **Sanford J. Grossman**. The analysis connects residual control rights with investment incentives and the boundaries of firms. Preserving Grossman's coauthorship matters even though the prize was awarded to Hart and Holmström. [@grossman-hart-1986]

The programs answer related but distinct questions. One asks how rewards should depend on available performance information. The other asks who should decide when an enforceable agreement leaves something unspecified. Neither assumes that every conflict can be solved by measuring one more outcome. [@ceremony-2016]

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented risk-neutral worker choosing effort $e\geq0$. Output is $y=e+\varepsilon$, where the disturbance has mean zero. The worker receives $a+by$ and incurs effort cost $e^2/2$. Holding the fixed payment $a$ constant, expected payoff is $a+be-e^2/2$.

The worker chooses $e=b$ at an interior optimum. If each unit of output is worth one to the employer and risk is ignored, stronger incentives can raise effort. This result belongs to the deliberately simple example; it does not establish the optimal reward for a risk-averse worker or a job with several tasks.

Now suppose the worker can devote attention to both measured output and unmeasured quality. Raising $b$ may shift attention away from quality. The original calculation omitted that effect, so it cannot settle the revised problem.

For control rights, imagine a supplier making a specialized investment before a buyer and supplier renegotiate their collaboration. If the supplier expects to receive only a small share of the resulting gains, it may invest too little. Ownership can change bargaining positions, but transferring control may also weaken the buyer's incentive to make its own investment. These are original teaching scenarios, not quantitative conclusions from the laureates' papers.

## The core economics

The worker's incentive problem in the single-task example is

$$
\max_{e\geq0}\left\{a+be-\frac{e^2}{2}\right\}.
$$

Differentiation gives $b-e=0$, so $e=b$ when $b\geq0$. The fixed payment affects participation but not the marginal return to effort in this risk-neutral, unconstrained setting.

With risk aversion and a noisy performance measure, compensation must also allocate risk. Information about common market conditions can help distinguish the worker's action from luck, subject to what is contractible and informative. The informativeness principle is a conditional result about information, not a demand to include every available variable in pay. [@press-2016]

A teaching incomplete-contract problem can be summarized as

$$
\max_{i\geq0}\{\lambda(o)S(i)-k(i)\},
$$

where $i$ is an investment, $S(i)$ the surplus it creates, $k(i)$ its cost and $\lambda(o)$ the investor's anticipated share under ownership arrangement $o$. An interior private choice satisfies

$$
\lambda(o)S'(i)=k'(i).
$$

Compared with a social marginal condition $S'(i)=k'(i)$, a share below one can weaken investment. This stripped-down construction holds the share fixed and omits the other party's investment. Hart's broader analysis compares those interacting incentives rather than choosing ownership from this one equation alone.

## What changed as a result

Contract theory provided a framework for analyzing compensation, insurance, corporate ownership and the organization of services. The academy emphasizes applications ranging from executive rewards to bankruptcy and public versus private provision. Those applications require their own evidence and institutional details. [@press-2016]

The framework also changes how a contract is evaluated. A low observed payment or a high output number is not enough. The analyst asks how the rule changes actions, which risks it transfers, which tasks it neglects and who can decide when unforeseen circumstances arise.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

Formal contracting models often simplify motivation, relationships, bargaining and legal enforcement. People can care about professional standards and fairness as well as money, while real organizations may struggle to measure the variables the model assumes available.

Ownership can affect power and distribution alongside efficiency. A theory of investment incentives does not by itself settle democratic accountability or service quality. This draft requires original-paper reading, empirical applications and independent review before it becomes a complete scholarly explanation.

## Key takeaways

- Rewards change both incentives and the allocation of risk.
- An informative measure can help separate effort from luck.
- Strong incentives on one task can damage unmeasured tasks.
- Incomplete agreements make control rights economically important.
- Optimal contracts depend on what can be observed and enforced.
