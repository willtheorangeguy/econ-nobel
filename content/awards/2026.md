## Abstract

Robert E. Hall and Michael Woodford received the 2026 prize for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilization. Hall's consumption research showed how forward-looking decisions imply restrictions that can be tested in data. Woodford developed a framework connecting household decisions, firms' price setting and interest-rate policy, including the role of expectations and commitment. The result provides a way to analyze stabilization through behavior rather than fixed aggregate correlations alone. Its predictions remain conditional on frictions, information and institutions. This essay uses the October announcement and scientific background; the December award ceremony has not yet occurred. [@press-2026] [@academy-2026]

## In plain English

A household deciding how much to spend considers more than this month's income. It may save a temporary payment, borrow against expected future earnings or change its spending when borrowing costs change. Some households cannot borrow and respond much more directly to current cash.

A firm setting a price also considers the future. If it cannot change the price again immediately, expected costs and demand over the period matter. Today's inflation can therefore depend on beliefs about tomorrow as well as current conditions.

Policy influences these decisions through actual actions and credible expectations about later actions. An announced interest-rate path can matter before every rate change occurs. But an announcement that people do not believe need not produce the same response. The academy emphasizes these forward-looking mechanisms in the new stabilization framework. [@academy-2026]

## The problem before this work

Historical correlations among income, consumption, inflation and unemployment can change when policy changes the behavior underlying them. A fitted relationship is not necessarily a stable rule for evaluating a new intervention.

The experience of the 1970s intensified this problem. The academy places the awarded work in a reconstruction of stabilization analysis that retained market imperfections while modeling households and firms as purposeful, forward-looking decision makers. The resulting theory could investigate policy feedback rather than assume it away. [@academy-2026]

It also needed to explain how monetary policy can affect real activity when individuals respond rationally. Frictions in price and wage adjustment create a role for demand and stabilization, while expectations influence the size and timing of responses. Rational decision making alone does not imply frictionless markets or an absence of policy effects.

## What the laureates contributed

**Hall** derived testable implications from consumption smoothing. His 1978 analysis used the restrictions implied by forward-looking choice rather than requiring researchers to observe every household expectation directly. The academy also describes subsequent household research with **Frederic Mishkin**, including consumers unable to smooth fully. Those later contributions prevent the benchmark from being misread as a claim that every household behaves identically. [@academy-2026] [@hall-1978]

**Woodford** developed central parts of the New Keynesian stabilization framework and synthesized the program in *Interest and Prices*. Price-setting frictions and imperfect competition coexist with optimizing behavior, enabling analysis of interest-rate rules and welfare. The academy explicitly credits many building blocks and Woodford's collaboration with **Julio Rotemberg**; the framework was not one person's solitary invention. [@academy-2026]

Their contributions were developed independently and at different times. The shared award connects household consumption foundations with the broader policy framework, rather than attributing every component to a joint Hall–Woodford paper. [@academy-2026]

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented household with two remaining periods, no interest, no uncertainty and unrestricted borrowing or saving. Its income is 100 in each period, and it prefers equal consumption across them. A one-time extra payment of 20 raises total resources from 200 to 220, so equal consumption rises from 100 to 110 in each period.

A household unable to borrow or facing urgent unmet needs can respond differently. The simple smoothing result is a benchmark under the stated conditions, not a universal marginal propensity to consume. Financing the payment can also change future taxes and resources, which this isolated example holds outside the calculation.

For a separate interest-rate example, let the nominal rate be 4 percent and expected inflation 2 percent. The approximate expected real rate is 2 percent. If expected inflation becomes 3 percent with the nominal rate unchanged, the approximate real rate falls to 1 percent.

The same nominal policy setting can therefore imply different incentives depending on expectations. These figures do not establish an optimal rate or a predicted output change. They are original teaching illustrations, not estimates from Hall, Woodford or the 2026 award background.

## The core economics

A household able to shift consumption across dates can satisfy an Euler condition

$$
u'(C_t)=\beta(1+r_t)E_t[u'(C_{t+1})],
$$

where $C_t$ is consumption, $u'$ marginal utility, $\beta$ discounting and $r_t$ a known real return for the relevant saving choice. With uncertainty in returns, the expectation must generally include the return as well.

Under special assumptions such as quadratic utility, a constant return and $\beta(1+r)=1$, this can imply $E_t[C_{t+1}]=C_t$. The random-walk consumption benchmark is not the general implication for every utility function or borrowing-constrained household. [@academy-2026]

A teaching New Keynesian demand relation is

$$
x_t=E_tx_{t+1}-\sigma^{-1}(i_t-E_t\pi_{t+1}-r_t^n),
$$

where $x_t$ is an output gap, $i_t$ the nominal rate, $\pi$ inflation, $r_t^n$ a natural real rate and $\sigma>0$ a sensitivity parameter in this convention. A pricing relation can take the form

$$
\pi_t=\beta E_t\pi_{t+1}+\kappa x_t+v_t,
$$

with $\kappa>0$ and a cost-pressure disturbance $v_t$. A policy rule completes the system. Expectations, equilibrium selection and the shock process must be specified to solve it.

These are explanatory summaries, not a full estimated model or a guarantee that a particular interest-rate rule stabilizes every shock. A cost disturbance can create trade-offs different from a pure demand disturbance.

## What changed as a result

The research supplied a behavioral foundation for analyzing policy rules, expectations and stabilization. The academy describes widespread use of related models in central banks and governments. Adoption is evidence of influence, not proof that each specification or historical intervention was correct. [@academy-2026]

The practical question becomes how a proposed action changes households' and firms' decisions, including their expectations about future policy. Temporary actions, lasting rules and credible commitments need not have the same effects even when today's budget or interest rate initially looks similar.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

Representative-household benchmarks can miss liquidity constraints, inequality and differences in spending responses. Financial frictions, learning and imperfect credibility can also change policy transmission. Building a model from individual optimization does not guarantee that its parameters remain invariant under every institutional change.

Welfare evaluation depends on which distortions and people the model includes. This newly announced award needs fuller original-text consultation and assessment of model evidence. Independent scholarly review remains outstanding; no future lecture or ceremony is treated as an available source.

## Key takeaways

- Consumption depends on expected resources and constraints across dates.
- Price-setting frictions can make monetary policy affect real activity.
- Expectations influence the effects of present and future policy.
- A behavioral model still needs empirical validation and credible assumptions.
- Stabilization results depend on the shock, institutions and welfare objective.
