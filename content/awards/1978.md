## Abstract

Herbert A. Simon's prize recognized research on decision making within economic organizations. Instead of assuming that a firm acts through one fully informed optimizer, he examined people with limited information, attention and computational capacity operating through communication and authority structures. Bounded rationality concerns those limits; satisficing describes searching for an acceptable alternative rather than guaranteeing the best possible one. The research changes both what economists ask about firms and what evidence they need. A rule that makes decisions manageable can be valuable while also creating systematic blind spots. [@ceremony-1978] [@simon-lecture-1978]

## In plain English

Suppose a hospital needs a new scheduling system. Staff cannot inspect every conceivable design, foresee every future emergency or calculate every consequence for patients and employees. They set requirements, examine alternatives, consult specialists and stop when they find a workable option before the deadline.

Calling that decision merely irrational misses the problem. Searching and calculation require time and resources. A realistic explanation needs to describe how alternatives are generated, what counts as acceptable and who has authority to decide. Simon's approach puts that process inside the theory instead of treating it as a detail after the optimal answer has been calculated.

The academy emphasized cooperation among decision makers and the coexistence of partly conflicting goals. Simon's lecture discusses organizations as ways of coping with limits on understanding and computation. This is not a claim that organizations always cope well; their structure can determine both what they notice and what they overlook. [@ceremony-1978] [@simon-lecture-1978]

## The problem before this work

A standard market model can represent a firm as choosing a profit-maximizing output. Such a simplification can be useful for a particular question about markets, even if it does not describe meetings, budgets or internal negotiation. The mistake is to treat that convenient representation as a complete explanation of actual organizational decisions.

The presentation speech explains why internal processes became especially important as ownership and management separated and organizations grew more complex. It contrasts a single all-knowing entrepreneur with cooperating people who have incomplete knowledge and social relationships. [@ceremony-1978]

Merely adding a cost of information does not automatically solve the difficulty. An optimizer deciding exactly how much information to gather may itself require knowledge of all possible future information and its value. Simon's agenda asks how workable procedures can be specified and tested without quietly reintroducing unlimited computation at a higher level. [@simon-lecture-1978]

## What the laureates contributed

**Simon was the sole recipient.** His work treated organizations as systems of people, tasks, communication and authority. The academy highlights *Administrative Behavior*, first published in 1947, and subsequent research on how decisions are actually made. [@ceremony-1978]

Bounded rationality describes limits on knowledge and calculation rather than a claim that people have no reasons for their actions. Satisficing is one response: search until an alternative meets an aspiration or acceptability standard. Other responses include dividing work among specialists, replacing broad objectives with operational subgoals and relying on routines. Simon's lecture explains these as related ways of making difficult decisions tractable. [@simon-lecture-1978]

The research program also included empirical investigation. Simon acknowledges work with colleagues and discusses organizational field studies, including research developed by Richard Cyert and James March. The award's focus on Simon does not make all behavioral theories of the firm his sole creation. [@simon-lecture-1978]

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented purchasing manager evaluating suppliers in sequence. The manager requires acceptable safety documentation, delivery within ten days and a price no higher than 100 units. Each evaluation costs 2 units of staff time. Suppliers appear in this order:

- A offers a price of 95 but delivery in twelve days.
- B offers a price of 98, delivery in eight days and acceptable documentation.
- C offers a price of 90, delivery in seven days and acceptable documentation.

A satisficing rule rejects A and accepts B, stopping after two evaluations. C would have been better on the listed dimensions, but the rule does not examine it. Evaluation costs total 4 rather than 6 if all three were checked.

This does not prove that stopping at B was optimal: the extra saving from C exceeds the extra evaluation cost in this particular list. The example deliberately shows the difference between a feasible decision procedure and a hindsight optimum. Before searching, the manager does not necessarily know C exists.

Changing the order of candidates or tightening the price threshold changes the outcome. That makes the search procedure, aspiration levels and organizational context empirically meaningful. A theory that merely says “choose something satisfactory” without specifying them would explain too little.

## The core economics

Let an alternative $x$ have observable attributes $q_1(x),\ldots,q_k(x)$ and minimum acceptable levels $a_1,\ldots,a_k$. After orienting each attribute so higher means better, define

$$
S=\{x:q_j(x)\geq a_j\ \text{for every }j\}.
$$

A simple sequential satisficing rule chooses the first encountered alternative in $S$. For a price ceiling of 100, the relevant inequality is equivalently $-\text{price}(x)\geq-100$. Some constraints can be hard requirements and others revisable aspirations.

This is an original teaching formulation, not a quotation of Simon's 1955 model. It makes three components explicit: the alternatives encountered, the criteria and the stopping rule. None is determined by the set notation itself.

Contrast that with

$$
x^*\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x\in X} v(x),
$$

where $X$ contains all feasible alternatives and $v$ assigns a common value. The optimization formulation can be useful, but it requires enough knowledge or structure to identify the relevant optimum. Simon's concern is how behavior works when discovering $X$, evaluating consequences or constructing $v$ is itself part of the problem. [@simon-lecture-1978]

## What changed as a result

Simon's work made the decision process a subject of economic explanation. Attention, search, communication and organizational structure could be investigated rather than hidden inside a single choice function. The academy emphasized applications to business and public administration and the foundation this provided for empirical research. [@ceremony-1978]

For practical analysis, the implication is to inspect the procedure. Who selects the options? Which indicators become targets? What information reaches the person with authority? A firm can respond differently to the same prices when those arrangements differ.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

Bounded rationality is a broad research program, not one precise behavioral law. A useful application must specify limits and rules closely enough to make predictions that could fail. Otherwise almost any outcome could be rationalized after the fact by inventing an aspiration level.

Simplifying a decision can also create organizational distortions. Meeting a departmental target may undermine the larger purpose; Simon's lecture discusses subgoal identification and its dependence on knowledge and power. Conversely, limited computation does not show that optimization is useless as a benchmark. This draft still needs original-paper reading and independent review of the historical and formal account. [@simon-lecture-1978]

## Key takeaways

- Information, search and calculation are limited resources.
- Satisficing is a specified stopping procedure, not a synonym for carelessness.
- Organizations distribute attention and knowledge as well as tasks.
- Local targets can help decisions while distorting broader objectives.
- A behavioral account needs observable rules and evidence, not only plausible stories.
