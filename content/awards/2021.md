## Abstract

David Card received half the prize for empirical contributions to labor economics; Joshua D. Angrist and Guido W. Imbens shared the other half for methodological contributions to analysis of causal relationships. Natural experiments exploit externally generated variation rather than deliberate researcher assignment. Card used such comparisons to study minimum wages, immigration and education. Angrist and Imbens clarified the interpretation of instrumental-variable estimates when treatment effects differ across people. Together the research strengthened causal analysis while making its assumptions and limits more explicit. A policy change is not automatically a valid experiment simply because it occurred outside the researcher’s control. [@press-2021]

## In plain English

People who obtain more education often earn more, but education may not be the only reason. Family resources, prior skills and other opportunities influence both schooling and earnings. A raw comparison mixes those differences with the effect of education itself.

A natural experiment looks for a change that affects treatment without creating the same differences in other relevant causes. A policy threshold, administrative rule or outside event can provide such variation, but the validity of the comparison must be argued from the setting.

Some people will respond to a policy while others would make the same choice either way. An estimate based on that policy may therefore describe the people whose treatment changed, rather than every person in society. The academy identifies this interpretation problem as central to Angrist and Imbens's contribution. [@ceremony-2021] [@press-2021]

## The problem before this work

Observed treatment is often selected. Employers, students and migrants respond to conditions that also affect outcomes. Statistical adjustment helps only if its assumptions account for the relevant confounding; placing controls in a regression does not itself create random assignment.

A comparison over time has another problem. Employment after a wage-law change differs from employment before it for many reasons. A comparison group can help isolate common changes, provided its untreated trend is a credible counterfactual for the treated group.

Instrumental variables add a third issue. A policy or encouragement can shift actual treatment without moving everyone. With heterogeneous effects, a single coefficient needs an explicit population interpretation. Otherwise the analyst may mistakenly describe a local effect as a universal return to treatment.

## What the laureates contributed

**Card** developed influential empirical studies using natural experiments in labor economics. The academy highlights minimum wages, immigration and education. His minimum-wage research with **Alan B. Krueger** compared fast-food establishments around New Jersey's 1992 increase with establishments in eastern Pennsylvania. The paper's opening states that they found no indication of reduced employment in that study. This is a setting-specific result, not a theorem that every wage increase has no employment cost. [@card-krueger-1994]

**Angrist** and **Imbens** clarified identification and estimation of local average treatment effects. Their framework explains what an instrumental-variable comparison can establish under assumptions including independence, exclusion and monotonicity. [@imbens-angrist-1994]

The shared methodological shift is to organize empirical analysis around a credible source of variation and a defined causal question. It does not eliminate uncertainty, replace knowledge of institutions or make all observational designs equivalent to randomized trials. [@press-2021]

## A concrete worked example

Consider invented employment indices in a treated and a comparison region. Both start at 100. After a policy change, the treated region reaches 102 while the comparison region falls to 97. The difference in changes is $2-(-3)=5$ index points.

That number estimates an effect only if the comparison region's three-point decline is a credible account of what would have happened to the treated region without the policy. A simultaneous regional shock or spillover across the border could invalidate that interpretation. The arithmetic alone cannot establish parallel untreated trends.

For a separate instrumental-variable example, imagine an externally assigned training encouragement that raises participation by 0.2 and average annual earnings by 2,000 monetary units. The ratio is $2{,}000/0.2=10{,}000$.

Under the required assumptions for a binary encouragement and binary training treatment, that ratio describes an average effect for people whose participation was induced by the encouragement. It need not describe always-participants, never-participants or a different policy's participants.

These are original teaching figures, not estimates from Card, Angrist or Imbens. The second example also assumes the encouragement affects earnings only through the defined training treatment, rather than through motivation, other assistance or employer contacts.

## The core economics

A two-group, two-period difference-in-differences contrast is

$$
\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{DiD}}
=(\overline Y_{T,1}-\overline Y_{T,0})
-(\overline Y_{C,1}-\overline Y_{C,0}).
$$

$T$ denotes the treated group, $C$ the comparison group, and 0 and 1 the dates. Identification requires an appropriate parallel-trends condition for untreated potential outcomes, alongside other design assumptions. Similar observed trends before treatment can support an argument but do not prove what the unobserved future trend would have been.

For binary instrument $Z$ and treatment $D$, the simple Wald ratio is

$$
\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{IV}}
=\frac{E[Y\mid Z=1]-E[Y\mid Z=0]}
{E[D\mid Z=1]-E[D\mid Z=0]}.
$$

The denominator must be nonzero. With independent assignment, exclusion, a well-defined treatment and monotonicity, the ratio identifies a local average treatment effect for compliers: people with $D(1)>D(0)$. Monotonicity rules out people who would move in the opposite treatment direction because of the instrument.

Those assumptions do substantial work. A weak first-stage effect undermines precision, while an exclusion violation can destroy the causal interpretation. These teaching formulas do not cover every modern design with multiple dates or continuously varying treatments. [@imbens-angrist-1994]

## What changed as a result

The research expanded empirical economics' ability to ask causal questions with observational data. The academy describes influence well beyond labor economics. It also made the population and assumptions behind an estimate more visible rather than treating a coefficient as a context-free law. [@press-2021]

For readers, a causal claim should identify the variation, the comparison and who responds to it. That information helps explain why two credible studies can estimate different effects without either being arithmetically wrong.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

Confidence intervals must accompany estimates. Natural experiments can be vulnerable to anticipation, sorting, concurrent policies and spillovers. A convincing story about an instrument must address alternative pathways to the outcome, not merely show that it predicts treatment.

Local effects can be highly policy-relevant but need not transfer to other populations or larger interventions. This draft requires fuller original-study consultation, sensitivity evidence and independent review before it becomes a complete scholarly explanation.

## Key takeaways

- Correlation can reflect selection rather than treatment effects.
- Natural experiments require a credible comparison argument.
- Difference-in-differences depends on untreated trend assumptions.
- Instrumental-variable effects can apply to a specific complier population.
- Identification and external validity are distinct research questions.
