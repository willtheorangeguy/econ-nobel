## Abstract

James J. Heckman and Daniel L. McFadden shared the prize for methods used to analyze microdata. Heckman examined selective samples and related problems of unobserved differences and evaluation. McFadden developed methods for discrete choices grounded in economic behavior. Both contributions address the gap between the records a researcher observes and the decisions that generated them. A large dataset does not eliminate that gap. The methods provide structured ways to learn under explicit assumptions, not automatic causal answers whenever a regression or choice model can be fitted. [@ceremony-2000] [@academy-2000]

## In plain English

A wage survey usually records wages for people who work. Those people may differ from people who do not work in ways the researcher cannot observe. Comparing their wages as though they were a random sample of everyone's potential offers can therefore mislead.

A transport survey poses a different problem. People choose a car, a bus or a train rather than a continuously divisible quantity of one transport mode. A model needs to connect the attributes of the alternatives and the person's circumstances with the probability of each choice.

Heckman and McFadden made those decision processes part of econometric analysis. The academy emphasizes the interaction between theory, statistical development and applications. The data are informative because a model explains what their selection and choice patterns mean—not simply because there are many rows. [@ceremony-2000]

## The problem before this work

Selection can arise through work, education, program participation or the availability of an observed outcome. If the same unobserved characteristics influence both selection and the outcome, the observed sample does not behave like a random draw from the population of interest.

Controlling for recorded characteristics may not remove the problem. Someone's unobserved opportunities or motivation can influence both entry into a program and later earnings. The relevant counterfactual outcome is not directly observed for that person under both choices.

Discrete choice requires its own statistical structure. Simply assigning numerical codes to bus, car and train and treating their average as a meaningful quantity loses the alternatives' economic interpretation. A useful model links choices to costs, time and other attributes while allowing for influences the researcher does not observe. The academy credits McFadden's development of that connection. [@ceremony-2000] [@academy-2000]

## What the laureates contributed

**Heckman** developed theory and methods for analyzing selective samples, with wider contributions to unobserved heterogeneity and evaluation. The academy discusses labor supply, education, labor-market programs and discrimination among the applications. The central insight is that observing an outcome can itself reflect an economic choice related to the outcome's determinants. [@ceremony-2000]

**McFadden** developed methods for discrete choice with foundations in economic theory. The academy highlights travel-mode choice and the BART application, along with other settings involving housing, services and environmental valuation. Those references establish the scope of his applied work; this draft has not independently evaluated the predictive accuracy of each study. [@ceremony-2000]

The shared prize recognizes distinct methodological programs. A sample-selection model and a multinomial choice model do not solve the same problem, and neither removes the need to specify credible behavior, information and data-generating assumptions.

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented travel-choice model with a car and a bus. Let the car's observed utility index be $\ln(3)$ and the bus's zero. Under a simple logit specification, the car probability is $3/(3+1)=0.75$, and the bus probability is 0.25.

Now add a second bus alternative with the same observed utility index. The model gives the car probability $3/(3+1+1)=0.60$, with 0.20 for each bus. Total bus probability becomes 0.40.

The calculation exposes an assumption rather than predicting what real travelers will do. If the second bus is nearly identical to the first, people may regard it as a close substitute for the existing bus rather than a new independent competitor to the car. A simple logit can then shift too much share away from the car because it treats unobserved utility components in a restrictive way.

This is an original teaching example. It does not show that discrete-choice modeling fails; it shows why the error structure and similarity among alternatives matter. More flexible models can allow richer substitution patterns, but they also require data and identification.

## The core economics

Write a latent outcome as

$$
y=x^\top\beta+\varepsilon,
$$

but suppose it is observed only if

$$
D=\mathbf{1}(z^\top\gamma+u>0).
$$

$x$ and $z$ are observed characteristics, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ parameters, $\varepsilon$ and $u$ unobserved influences, and $D$ the selection indicator. If the disturbances are related, the selected sample can have a nonzero conditional mean of $\varepsilon$.

Under a particular jointly normal error model with the variance of $u$ normalized to one and errors independent of the recorded covariates,

$$
\operatorname{E}[y\mid x,z,D=1]
=x^\top\beta+\rho\sigma_\varepsilon\lambda(z^\top\gamma).
$$

$\rho$ is the disturbance correlation, $\sigma_\varepsilon$ the outcome-error standard deviation and $\lambda(a)=\phi(a)/\Phi(a)$ the inverse Mills ratio, using the standard normal density and distribution function. This teaching formula illustrates a selection correction under strong distributional assumptions. It is not a correction guaranteed to work for arbitrary missing data.

For the choice illustration, utilities are $U_j=V_j+\eta_j$. With independent identically distributed type-I extreme-value errors at a normalized scale, the multinomial logit probability is

$$
P(j)=\frac{e^{V_j}}{\sum_k e^{V_k}}.
$$

$V_j$ is the observed index for alternative $j$; $\eta_j$ the unobserved component. The assumptions generate the formula and its substitution restrictions. These equations are explanatory presentations, not quotations of the original publications.

## What changed as a result

The research made economic decisions about observation and choice integral to statistical analysis. The academy describes the resulting methods as central tools of microeconometrics, developed alongside substantive applications. [@ceremony-2000]

For a reader, the practical questions are who is observed, why they are observed and which alternatives were actually available. A coefficient or predicted choice share is interpretable only in relation to those processes. More observations can improve precision without repairing a mistaken model of selection or choice.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

Selection correction depends on identifying assumptions. Functional form alone may provide fragile identification; credible variables that affect selection without directly changing the outcome can be valuable, but their exclusion requires an economic argument. Program effects can also differ across people, so one estimated average need not generalize universally.

Choice models face analogous problems with omitted attributes, unavailable alternatives and unobserved correlation. The logit example deliberately makes one restriction visible rather than presenting its probabilities as facts. This draft requires original-paper consultation, fuller empirical discussion and independent review before it is a completed scholarly account.

## Key takeaways

- Observed outcomes may come from a selected rather than random sample.
- Unobserved factors can influence both selection and the outcome.
- Discrete-choice probabilities depend on a model of utilities and errors.
- Similar alternatives can require correlated or richer error structures.
- Statistical estimation does not replace identification or causal reasoning.
