## Abstract

Eugene F. Fama, Lars Peter Hansen and Robert J. Shiller shared the prize for empirical analysis of asset prices. Fama investigated how quickly prices incorporate information and how returns relate to risk. Shiller challenged simple pricing models through evidence on volatility and longer-horizon predictability. Hansen developed generalized method of moments, enabling researchers to confront theoretical restrictions with data. Together their contributions show why unpredictable short-run movements, predictable expected returns and disagreement about market efficiency can coexist. The award did not certify a universal forecasting rule or settle every debate between rational and behavioral explanations. [@ceremony-2013] [@academy-2013]

## In plain English

An asset is valuable because of what owning it can deliver later. A share may produce dividends and a resale value; a bond promises payments subject to its terms and risks. Today's price depends on expectations about those outcomes and how people value money across dates and states of the world.

Unexpected news can therefore move a price immediately. If an increase were safely predictable and costlessly exploitable, buying pressure could bring it forward. But uncertainty makes that simple argument incomplete: an asset can offer a higher expected return as compensation for bearing risk.

The distinction matters when interpreting evidence. A statistical tendency for returns to differ across periods is not automatically a riskless profit opportunity. Nor does the difficulty of forecasting tomorrow's movement prove that today's price is socially appropriate. The academy's scientific account emphasizes risk, statistical inference and the absence of a fully accepted explanation of all price behavior. [@academy-2013]

## The problem before this work

Testing an asset price requires a benchmark for the payments it is expected to generate and how those payments should be discounted. Future cash flows are uncertain, and the required compensation for risk may change.

A researcher observing a high price relative to dividends must therefore consider several possibilities: stronger expected future dividends, lower required returns, a different assessment of risk, or mispricing. Observing the ratio alone does not distinguish them.

Likewise, failure of a particular model can reflect its assumptions about preferences, consumption measurement, trading constraints or beliefs. It need not identify one unique alternative. A useful empirical program needs statistical tools and explicit economic restrictions, rather than a verbal assertion that prices are either always right or always irrational.

## What the laureates contributed

**Fama** developed influential empirical research on informational efficiency, including event studies and analysis of expected returns. The academy describes evidence that new public information is incorporated quickly and that short-horizon return predictability is limited. That is more careful than claiming that no return can ever be predicted. [@ceremony-2013] [@fama-1970]

**Shiller** investigated whether price volatility could be reconciled with simple models of discounted dividends and later studied longer-horizon predictability. His findings stimulated behavioral finance and debate over changing risk compensation. Excess volatility is defined relative to a model and its assumptions, not merely by noticing that a price graph looks turbulent. [@academy-2013]

**Hansen** developed generalized method of moments, or GMM. It estimates and tests model restrictions expressed as averages of suitable functions of the data. Applied to asset pricing, it helped evaluate consumption-based explanations without requiring a fully specified distribution for every observation. Its usefulness extends beyond finance. [@ceremony-2013] [@hansen-1982]

## A concrete worked example

Suppose an invented asset pays a certain 110 units one year from now. At a risk-free interest rate of 10 percent, its price is $110/1.10=100$. If that rate falls to 5 percent, its price becomes approximately $110/1.05=104.76$.

The payment did not change, yet the price rose. In this deliberately certain example, the change reflects discounting rather than optimism about dividends or investor error. Risky shares introduce further uncertainty and risk compensation, so the same arithmetic cannot directly explain a stock-market episode.

Now consider two risky assets with the same average payoff. One tends to pay badly when households already have little consumption; the other pays well in those states. Their average payoffs alone need not imply equal prices. The second asset offers valuable insurance and can command a higher price under suitable preferences.

These are original teaching examples, not estimated returns, advice about purchasing an asset or a reconstruction of the laureates' datasets. They illustrate why a pricing explanation needs more than average cash flows and why a changing valuation ratio has several possible causes.

## The core economics

A common one-period pricing representation is

$$
P_t=E_t[m_{t+1}X_{t+1}],
$$

where $P_t$ is today's price, $X_{t+1}$ the next-period total payoff, $E_t$ an expectation using current information, and $m_{t+1}$ a stochastic discount factor. The discount factor weights payoffs by time and state. A larger weight in bad times means payments received then are particularly valuable. [@academy-2013]

For a positive-price asset with gross return $R_{t+1}=X_{t+1}/P_t$, the corresponding restriction is

$$
E_t[m_{t+1}R_{t+1}-1]=0.
$$

A model specifies a discount factor, possibly depending on parameters $\theta$. With instruments $Z_t$ available at time $t$, it can imply unconditional moments

$$
E[Z_t\{m_{t+1}(\theta)R_{t+1}-1\}]=0.
$$

GMM uses sample counterparts to such restrictions. It selects parameters to make the moments close to zero according to a weighting rule. Valid inference requires conditions on the moments, dependence and identification; a small fitted discrepancy does not establish the truth of every economic assumption.

The equations are teaching summaries of the pricing-and-testing logic. They do not reproduce Hansen's full asymptotic argument or establish that one consumption model prices all assets correctly.

## What changed as a result

Asset-pricing research became a sustained confrontation between explicit models and empirical regularities. The academy describes influence on indexing and portfolio-management practice as well as academic theory. Such influence is part of the historical significance, not evidence that any particular investment strategy is always appropriate. [@ceremony-2013]

The three contributions also clarify why apparently conflicting findings can be informative together. Limited short-run predictability and changing longer-run expected returns concern different questions. Statistical rejection of a simple rational benchmark motivates further models; it does not uniquely prove a psychological explanation.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

Predictability can be unstable, sensitive to sample periods or exaggerated by trying many specifications. Trading costs and constraints can separate a statistical pattern from a feasible strategy. Long-horizon observations may also provide fewer independent episodes than the raw number of dates suggests.

Risk-based and behavioral accounts remain subject to measurement and identification problems. This draft requires original-paper consultation, precise variance-bound assumptions, fuller treatment of joint testing and independent review before it is complete.

## Key takeaways

- Prices depend on expected payoffs and how those payoffs are valued.
- Short-run unpredictability does not prove perfect pricing.
- Predictable expected returns can reflect changing risk compensation.
- GMM connects economic restrictions with statistical evidence.
- Rejecting one pricing model does not identify a unique alternative.
