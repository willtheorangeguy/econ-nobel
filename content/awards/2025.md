## Abstract

Joel Mokyr received half the prize for identifying prerequisites for sustained growth through technological progress. Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt shared the other half for the theory of sustained growth through creative destruction. Mokyr's historical approach examines the interaction of useful knowledge, understanding and openness to change. Aghion and Howitt model innovation that improves technology while displacing earlier producers. Together the research asks why innovation can continue rather than remain an isolated event. The resulting growth can create large gains and concentrated losses, so aggregate progress does not eliminate political conflict or the need for adjustment. [@press-2025]

## In plain English

An invention can improve life once without producing a continuing process of improvement. Sustained growth requires people to build on discoveries, explain failures, share useful knowledge and have reasons to develop something better.

A new technology can also threaten existing businesses. A more efficient producer may replace one whose investment was valuable under the older method. Innovation is creative because it expands possibilities and destructive because some existing products, firms or jobs lose value.

The academy connects these mechanisms with both historical prerequisites and formal growth theory. It also emphasizes that progress can be blocked by established interests. A society's ability to support change and manage its costs is therefore part of the explanation, not merely a consequence after growth has occurred. [@ceremony-2025] [@press-2025]

## The problem before this work

Human history contains important inventions long before modern sustained growth. Explaining one improvement is therefore different from explaining a process in which improvements regularly generate further improvements.

Accumulating more capital can raise output, but diminishing returns can limit that mechanism when knowledge is fixed. An account of continuing productivity growth needs to explain technological change and the economic decisions behind it.

Innovation also complicates welfare analysis. A successful entrant creates a better product but may take profits from an incumbent. The private reward can omit knowledge spillovers while including gains that partly transfer business from someone else. These opposing effects mean that the private incentive to innovate need not coincide with the social incentive. A model must specify the mechanisms before concluding that research is always too low or always too high.

## What the laureates contributed

**Mokyr** used historical analysis to investigate the prerequisites for sustained technological progress. The academy emphasizes connections between practical know-how and explanations of why techniques work, alongside social openness to new ideas. His contribution concerns the development and accessibility of useful knowledge, not a claim that every useful technique must await a complete scientific theory. [@press-2025]

**Aghion and Howitt** jointly developed a mathematical model of growth through creative destruction. The checked 1992 article provides a foundational entry into this program. Innovative research changes the quality of production and the position of incumbent firms, bringing competition and technological replacement into long-run growth analysis. [@aghion-howitt-1992]

The historical and formal programs are complementary but use different evidence and methods. The shared award does not mean that all three authors wrote one model or that a formal growth equation proves the entire historical explanation. All three recipients' distinct contributions should remain visible. [@ceremony-2025]

## A concrete worked example

Consider an invented technology whose productivity rises by 10 percent each time a successful innovation arrives. If innovations arrive at an average rate of 0.2 per year, expected log productivity grows at approximately $0.2\log(1.1)=0.01906$, or 1.91 percent per year.

The growth rate is not a promise that productivity rises smoothly by that amount every year. Individual breakthroughs occur at discrete uncertain dates. A large collection of activities can look smoother than any one producer's experience.

Suppose a new method raises annual output value from 100 to 110 at unchanged resource use. The additional 10 is a gross gain in the simplified production comparison. If developing it costs resources, those costs must be counted. If it displaces a producer, losses of its profits are not automatically an additional destruction of the same amount of social output.

At the same time, displaced workers can experience real transition costs and long periods of lost earnings. A higher aggregate output figure does not show that those workers are compensated. The example separates a productivity mechanism from a full welfare and distribution assessment.

These are original teaching assumptions, not parameters or results estimated by the laureates. They do not establish an optimal research subsidy, competition rule or social-insurance policy. Such conclusions require additional mechanisms and evidence.

## The core economics

Let $q$ be a productivity level and let a successful innovation multiply it by $\gamma>1$:

$$
q_{\mathrm{new}}=\gamma q_{\mathrm{old}}.
$$

If innovations arrive according to a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$, expected log productivity grows at

$$
\frac{dE[\log q_t]}{dt}=\lambda\log\gamma.
$$

Expected productivity in levels grows at rate $\lambda(\gamma-1)$ in this particular construction. These two rates differ because expectation and the logarithm do not commute. The numerical example uses expected log growth, not an incorrectly identical level-growth measure.

The innovation rate must itself be explained. Research effort, expected profit, competition and the likelihood of being displaced by the next innovator can affect incentives. A successful incumbent may enjoy temporary gains while anticipating their eventual erosion.

A schematic private research condition compares the expected value of success with its marginal cost. Social evaluation also includes knowledge benefits to others, effects on consumers and displacement or adjustment costs. A profit earned by taking customers from another firm is not automatically equal to a new social gain.

These equations are a teaching representation of stepwise innovation, not the full equilibrium system of Aghion and Howitt's paper. They omit the labor allocation, pricing and research-market conditions required to solve that system. [@aghion-howitt-1992]

## What changed as a result

The research explains sustained growth as a process requiring institutions, knowledge and incentives rather than an automatic accumulation of inventions. It also places replacement and conflict inside growth theory, instead of treating them as incidental disturbances around a smooth trend. [@press-2025]

For readers, the policy question is how to preserve opportunities for improvement while addressing adjustment and resistance. Protecting a particular incumbent and protecting people exposed to change are different interventions. Their effects on innovation, distribution and welfare require explicit comparison rather than a universal prescription.

## Criticisms, caveats and unresolved questions

Historical explanations face difficulties separating culture, institutions, resources and international relationships. A conceptual distinction between know-how and understanding helps organize evidence but does not by itself establish the causal contribution of each factor.

Formal innovation models simplify research uncertainty, market power and the direction of technological change. Faster measured productivity growth is not a complete welfare criterion when environmental effects and distribution matter. This draft requires original-book and article consultation, broader evidence and independent review before it is complete.

## Key takeaways

- An isolated invention differs from a self-sustaining process of improvement.
- Useful knowledge depends on creation, understanding and access.
- Innovation can create productivity gains while displacing incumbents.
- Private research rewards and social gains need not coincide.
- Sustained growth requires attention to institutions and adjustment costs.
